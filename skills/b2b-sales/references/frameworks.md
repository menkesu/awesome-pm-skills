# B2B Sales - Frameworks

Grouped by theme. Each entry: originator and episode, what it is for, when it applies or fails, steps, benchmarks.

## 1. Choose the game, ICP and segment

**1. Deal size determines motion** (Jen Abel, Jen Abel 2.0) - Inbound vs outbound is decided by ACV. A $5K deal must be marketing-led; a $100K deal means outbound from day one. Steps: compute the return on a seller's time at your ACV; choose marketing-led for small ACV and sales-led outbound for large. Elena Verna puts the sales floor near $15K; below it, keep products self-serve.

**2. Pick your go-to-market game / the mid-market does not exist** (Jen Abel, 2.0 and 1.0) - SMB is a high-volume marketing game, enterprise a high-value sales game; mid-market is upper SMB or lower enterprise and straddling both loses. Steps: ask who you built this for and where the problem is felt most; decide which game excites you; hire people and set ACV to fit. Also: selling to 100 people vs 1,000 people is a different game and there is no hybrid.

**3. Match GTM motion to buyer/user structure** (Bret Taylor) - Developer-led works for platform products sold to engineers (Stripe, Twilio); PLG works when user and buyer are the same person; direct sales is needed when buyer and user differ or the buyer must be engaged. Many AI products need direct sales. Varun Mohan and Jason Lemkin agree that pure PLG is a trap for products needing security, deployment and onboarding effort. Counterpoint: Claire Butler (Figma) ran bottom-up with no sales team for three years.

**4. Be honest about your motion from the first 10-20 customers** (Jason Lemkin) - Look at how the first 10-20 unaffiliated customers actually bought. Paid by card with no talk: double down on self-serve. Need conversations (even at $5K): embrace sales. Many companies end up hybrid.

**5. Favorite customer / white-hot center ICP** (Jonathan Lowenhar) - Ask which existing customer would rave about you on the phone; reverse-engineer the ICP from them; add qualifications, discovery questions, kill criteria; defer adjacencies.

**6. Tight ICP at account and persona level** (Pete Kazanjy) - Define account characteristics plus personas (user, technical influencer, budget owner) and plan the path from first conversation to the budget owner. Tight ICP means more loops with people in the white-hot center.

**7. Kill criteria** (Jonathan Lowenhar; example set from Annie Duke) - Write the signals that mean do not sign. Duke's RFP/RFI examples: written with a competitor in mind (ask how far along they are); customer refuses a demo and only discusses price (kill); no decision maker after a few meetings (propose an executive alignment meeting, kill if refused). First-time founders say yes to everyone, which is the most expensive GTM mistake.

**8. Segmentation as a graph, derived from data** (Jeanne DeWitt Grosser) - X-axis size (buying complexity), Y-axis growth potential, then offering-specific cuts that change how you sell. Cap at about three attributes for a small team. In a new company, in 30 days ask data science which ex-ante attributes predict a $100K vs $1M customer and where you win repeatedly.

**9. Pick enterprise function by budget** (Jen Abel, 3.0) - Legal is the largest budget line (3-4x other budgets) and risk-driven: it wants the cheapest option or a high-risk problem you can land at $250-500K. Use when choosing which function to target.

**10. Go after tier-one logos early** (Jen Abel, 2.0) - Contrary to VC advice to learn downmarket, category leaders are the real early adopters because they take swings to stay number one; one landed logo is referenceability for the whole industry. Applies at the $1M-$10M ARR phase, especially AI. Fails if you cannot integrate with enterprise systems or lack enterprise sales experience. Ride the adjacent ring: use each logo as the opener to the next ring.

## 2. Crossing the chasm and adoption phase

**11. Crossing the Chasm** (Geoffrey Moore) - Visionaries buy without peers and will not serve as references for pragmatists, who buy what peers buy. Steps: win a marquee visionary; pick a beachhead segment you can lead; get 3-6 reputable companies in it to pick you. Applies to B2B disruptive technology; Moore says it is not designed for B2C.

**12. Technology adoption lifecycle: four playbooks** (Geoffrey Moore) - Early market (project model, we believe what you believe), bowling alley (solution model, we need what you have), tornado (land grab, we want what they have), Main Street (services innovation). Failure mode: clinging to the playbook that worked after the market moved (e.g. qualifying on budget before the tornado).

**13. Bowling alley and adjacent segments** (Geoffrey Moore) - Win a segment, then move to one adjacent via customer (same customer, new use case) or via partners (same use case, new customers). Non-adjacent moves break reference and partner leverage.

**14. Compelling reason to buy** (Geoffrey Moore) - Pragmatists act only under duress from a severe, worsening problem. Look for a sponsor whose boss's boss knows their name; anchor on the cost of not solving it. Not the same as a compelling reason to sell (a sexier demo).

**15. Lighthouse customer** (Geoffrey Moore) - A famous visionary customer gives you a story, not a business; it is usually not your beachhead. Nine of ten executive sponsors will route you to purchasing and process.

**16. Bonfire analogy** (Geoffrey Moore) - Taking any customer is a match run back and forth under a log. Hold the match on kindling (one narrow segment), win it, then expand adjacently.

**17. Shut the laptop: problem-first discovery** (Geoffrey Moore) - In the bowling alley the conversation is never about you. Open with: we have worked with others in your industry and understand there is this problem; do you have it? Run a diagnostic before presenting, take handwritten notes, build lead gen around symptoms. Fails in early market and tornado.

## 3. Outbound and first meetings

**18. Manual 30-lead experiment** (Jen Abel) - Find 30 people you want to learn from, spend 15-20 minutes on each note, count responses, adjust message or role, repeat. Learn parameters before using enrichment tools. If you cannot find 30, you have a targeting problem.

**19. Four-part cold note** (Jen Abel) - Relevant to the role, counterintuitive or different (not better), about the problem not the solution, short enough for a phone (3-4 sentences). Test with Gmail audio replay. Her first 20 Jellyfish customers came from cold email led with a counterintuitive belief.

**20. Pincer model** (Jen Abel, 3.0) - Target only the executive decision-maker (founder outreach) and an N-1 (AE outreach); when one replies, loop in the other for role parity; 2-3 sentences per touch. Applies to roughly $100K+ enterprise deals. Fails if you cannot excite an executive. Use an LLM to find likely targets, then verify.

**21. Alpha / sell the gap** (Jen Abel, 2.0 and 3.0) - Lead with what the executive uncovers and unlocks: the edge your product gives (data, speed, talent, access). Litmus test: what would they say about it at the board meeting that shows needle-moving impact? Gap selling over problem selling for leaders; when told they already have a tool, agree and reframe upstream instead of arguing.

**22. Manual, tool-free outbound for 100K+ deals** (Jen Abel, 2.0) - Hand-craft each note, tune tone to seniority, email people not in the databases everyone hits, tune the framing or subject line rather than the formulaic first line. Fails in high-volume, low-ACV motions.

**23. Cohort-based sales iteration** (Pete Kazanjy) - Run 10 first meetings at a time, watch how discovery, slides and demo land, add a slide or answer for any objection you could not handle, update collateral, run the next cohort.

**24. Research-report outreach** (Sahil Mansuri) - To break into an account that rejects every pitch, build a short report from your own proprietary data about them, send it to the exec with a subject line about the insight, lead with teaching. Needs data the buyer cannot get elsewhere. In tough budgets, over-index on teaching, not pitching.

**25. Text intros, keep the introducer on the thread** (Sahil Mansuri) - Warm referrals over text, not email; keep the introducer on for 10-20 messages so social pressure keeps the thread alive. Related: Jen Abel says six-figure deals close through text and relationship.

**26. Crisp use case to find the decision-maker** (Ayo Omojola) - One specific use case makes the person with their finger on the button obvious; go to them directly instead of networking through departments.

## 4. Calls, demos, pitch and objections

**27. The informal intro call** (Jen Abel, 3.0) - 30 minutes, no demo, no slides, no recorder. Let them speak first, ask what needs to change next year versus this year, how it is measured, why now, why not wait, what they want to own. Last ~10 minutes on what you do, framed to what they said. They will not tell you this again once it feels like a sales process.

**28. Ask for the no, and book call 2 on call 1** (Jen Abel) - Name the politeness: I'm getting the vibe it might not be a fit; did I misinterpret? Pull up calendars and book the second call with who else should be invited; 'I'll email you' is usually a no. Sahil Mansuri: great sellers are internally pessimistic and look for signals the deal will not close.

**29. Pre-demo call, demo the 20%, post-demo debrief** (Jen Abel, 3.0) - 15-minute champion call before the demo (who attends, what resonates, what to ask); demo only the 20% that delivers 80% of value to this buyer; text the champion right after for a five-minute debrief on who is skeptical. Upmarket, delay the demo; downmarket ($3K tools), demo fast.

**30. Setup + follow-through pitch** (April Dunford, 2.0) - Setup: market insight, pluses and minuses of the alternatives, perfect world criteria. Follow-through: introduce yourself, differentiated value shown as a demo organized by the criteria, proof, silent objections, the ask. More than half the call on differentiated value. Applies after about 10 deals; fails pre-traction. Opposes trend-based and new-vs-old pitches.

**31. Win the champion, then arm the champion** (April Dunford) - Of 5-7 people in a B2B buy, the champion who runs the evaluation matters most for positioning; once live, arm them with answers for IT, purchasing and users (compliance info, training plan, ROI calculator, how procurement works). Buyers need help justifying a short-list decision; 40-60% of B2B purchases end in no decision. Jen Abel disagrees on who to start with (executive and N-1).

**32. Best-rep pitch test** (April Dunford) - Train your best rep on the new pitch, test on qualified prospects (not existing customers, who are polluted by the old positioning), debrief after each pitch, pass when the rep will not go back, film them to evangelize to the team. Companies report doubling first-call-to-opportunity conversion by tightening the pitch.

**33. Demand-side sales / three demos** (Bob Moesta) - Ask where the buyer is in their timeline (first thought, passive looking, active looking, deciding, first use, ongoing use) and tailor the interaction. Autobooks split one demo into three (problem stories, alternatives, choices), halving the cycle and quadrupling conversion.

**34. Affirmation loops and create-the-need** (Madhavan Ramanujam) - Pause in demos so the customer plays back the value; ask about the current painful process and its duration, then what if it were instant.

**35. Reframe the laundry list** (Nan Yu) - When a buyer lists ten demands, ask whether all are equally important; win the top three with native depth and negotiate the rest.

**36. Be upfront about what you cannot do; start low-risk with data** (Jen Abel) - State today's capabilities, gaps and the next six months. For data products, start with a small low-risk subset, prove value, then connect more.

**37. Whiteboard discovery and add value at every touch** (Jeanne DeWitt Grosser) - Have the customer draw their architecture so they leave with an asset; give insight at every touchpoint because lost deals often return in the next buying cycle. Nan Yu: a no means not now, keep them in the CRM. Jen Abel: about 25% of lost deals boomerang within a year.

## 5. Pilots, POCs, procurement and closing

**38. Time-boxed pilot** (Jen Abel, 3.0) - Free 48-72 hours with 3-4 daily users plus champion, 3 tasks, co-authored success criteria; shortens the cycle by about two weeks. Integration-heavy products: paid 1-2 month pilot credited on signing. Reverse-engineer the deal first (signature date, procurement, security, legal).

**39. POC = business case; co-created ROI; charge smartly** (Madhavan Ramanujam) - Frame the POC goal as creating a business case. Co-create the ROI model from day one (incremental gains, cost savings, opportunity cost) so inputs are agreed; charge to qualify serious buyers and state the POC price is not an anchor. Opposes free technical-fit POCs.

**40. Commitment-qualifying technical questionnaire** (Raaz Herzberg) - When a buyer asks for a proof of value, send a long technical questionnaire; a fast thorough reply shows pull. Do not push an uncommitted buyer into a POV with an immature product.

**41. Papering and procurement playbook** (Jen Abel, 3.0 and 1.0) - Send a Word doc and ask whether to use your paper or theirs; email the agreed timeline with pricing contingent on a signing date; know the signer; do their forms; split technology and service contracts; do not start work until signed; procurement takes about 30 days. Procurement's job is to align the purchase with their process, not to kill the deal; getting stuck there usually means you sold too junior.

**42. Services-first, co-authored scope** (Jen Abel; Benedict Evans on enterprise capacity) - If the buyer has no process to adopt your technology, sell a 90-day service co-authoring the scope, then convert to the technology contract. Jen sees 40-50% of B2B SaaS companies needing this. Enterprises lack spare capacity to redesign workflows, so bundle implementation capacity. Fails if buyers already know how to buy the technology; FDEs break economics on $100K deals (Abel), and work only with real engineers on large tickets (Nabeel Qureshi).

**43. Enterprise deal crafting** (Jen Abel, 2.0) - From $1M to $10M, package your learnings and own the framing so perceived value far exceeds cost (an integration cheap for you, a speaking slot); the product is pricing, the opportunity and not letting them compare you to two other options.

## 6. Stalled deals and no-decision

**44. JOLT Effect** (Matt Dixon) - Judge indecision, Offer a recommendation, Limit exploration, Take risk off the table. 56% of no-decision losses are buyers who want to buy but fear failure; 87% of buyers show moderate or high indecision. Three fear drivers: right configuration, new information after signing, believable ROI.

**45. Challenger approach** (Matt Dixon) - Show the customer a risk or opportunity they do not know about and tie it to your unique benefit. Works on indifference; alone it does not fix indecision.

**46. Reset expectations and safety nets** (Matt Dixon) - Build the business case on what you hit in 100% of implementations; add post-signature roadmap with stage gates, owners, metrics; position services as insurance; offer carve-outs. Dunford adds: split the deal, money-back guarantee, teach them how to buy.

**47. Why deals slow down; pipeline problems are top of funnel** (Jen Abel) - Fascinated-but-slow means your buyer cannot sell it upward, you have not framed the problem's implications, or they are being nice. Stalls that look like bottom-of-funnel problems are qualification errors.

**48. Deal-bot and lost-bot** (Jeanne DeWitt Grosser) - Run an agent over call transcripts, email and Slack to find real loss reasons (Vercel's price loss was really no economic buyer). Jason Lemkin agrees: study losses more than wins.

**49. Internal champion monitoring** (Jen Abel) - The champion wanted this from day one and project-manages inside; silence is the risk signal. Use them to find skeptics. Lemkin: always leave with a logged next step.

## 7. Funnel benchmarks and measurement

**50. Stage-conversion leading indicators** (Pete Kazanjy) - Of 10 first meetings about 7 reach a second, 4.5 a third, 2-3 win. Missing second dates means the message or targeting is off. Focus on high quantity of high-quality actions, not the score.

**51. Enterprise funnel benchmarks** (Jen Abel, 3.0 and 1.0) - About half of qualified leads lost around the demo; about 25% advance beyond; post-pilot success about 80%; about 25% of losses boomerang within a year; 1 in 4 intro calls disqualified for maturity; cycles 6-12 months; sweet-spot initial ACV $50-200K; enterprise is about 15 buyer steps, not 5 CRM stages.

## 8. Hiring, handoff and comp

**52. Founder-led selling and the selling while loop** (Pete Kazanjy; echoed by Jen Abel, Jason Lemkin, Raaz Herzberg, Varun Mohan) - Founders sell the first couple dozen customers: feedback loop on problem, message and product. Run the loop on local first, extract to slides and scripts, hire two reps, then 4, 8, 16. Readiness: roughly 15-25% first-meeting-to-customer across 50-100 at-bats.

**53. A+ middlers and first-rep timing** (Jason Lemkin) - Founders are weak at opening and closing but excellent in the middle; learn to ask for next steps and money. Close the first 10 customers yourself; hire reps when sales takes more than 20% of your time; hire two to A/B test humans; two reps closing $1M each beat 20 closing $100K. Raaz Herzberg: after a couple million ARR; Jeanne Grosser: when you can write the ICP, around $1M ARR; Merci Grace: when you cannot meet demand or move upmarket.

**54. Playbook before GTM engineering** (Jeanne DeWitt Grosser) - Document the best practice (around 10 people), then have a GTM engineer shadow the top performer, encode the workflow into an agent, and keep a human reviewer until approval rates are high. Fails on deep multi-layer enterprise prospecting.

**55. Retention-aligned comp** (Sahil Mansuri) - Add a kicker when a rep's signed customer renews and compare each rep's renewal rate to the company. Mansuri admits few companies do this well.

**56. Adding sales to PLG: PQA, PQL, MQL paths and signals** (Elena Verna; Annie Pearl, Varun Parmar, Archie Abrams on hybrid) - Three lead paths with separate playbooks; do not hire sales until hand-raisers exist; 90% of product-led sales is finding the buyer outside the user base; alert on admin changes and terms/privacy page visits; Wizard-of-Oz the stack; hire reps matching the PQL motion and the real buyer (Annie Pearl); treat self-serve and sales as one product with two channels; unify LTV and attribution (Abrams).
