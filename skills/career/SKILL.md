# Career: Plan, Promotion Case, Job Move, Search and Negotiation

![career: lead guest team](assets/card.png)

Diagnose which career decision you are really facing, run the matching play, and leave with a one-page decision brief plus scripts you can send today. Built from 745 insights from 188 Lenny's Podcast guests. **Lead team:** Phyl Terry, Nikhyl Singhal, Deb Liu.

## When to use
- You feel stuck, restless or passed over and cannot tell whether to fix it, move teams or leave.
- You want a promotion case or a calibrated answer to 'why am I not at the next level?'.
- You are choosing between jobs, or deciding whether your company is a dead end (ex-growth, layoffs, no sponsor).
- You are laid off or starting a search and need a system, not a blast of applications.
- You have an offer (or are about to) and need a negotiation script and the right order of asks.
- You wonder whether to go IC, manager, founder or executive, or how AI changes what you should learn.
- You are an aspiring PM (support, sales, eng, design, ops) looking for the entry path.

## Step 1 - Diagnose (ask before answering)
If the user attached a resume, self-review, promo doc, offer letter, job description or org chart, read it first and infer. Then ask at most 4:
1. **Which decision is on the table?** Plan, promotion, stay-or-leave, search, offer, AI-era reinvention, break-in. Routes to the play in Step 2; most people name one and need two (a search needs Play A first).
2. **Level, years and track?** APM/PM, senior, lead, director+, IC or manager. Early careers optimize for learning and reps; leader transitions hit the 'optics' and 'what got you here' walls (Fareed Mosavat, Ethan Evans, Shreyas Doshi).
3. **Company state and your leverage?** Growing, ex-growth, laid off; employed with runway or urgent income need. The ability to say no is power, and haste is risk (Jacob Warwick).
4. **What do you want in one sentence, with a reason that is yours?** If the answer is 'a promotion' or 'VP', ask why until it turns internal (Ken Norton's laddering). If they do not know, start with Play A.

## Step 3 - Run it

### Play A: Find direction (Moesta, Skarstad, Weaver, Ken Norton)
1. **Energy audit (2 weeks).** After every meeting or project, mark it red (drained), yellow (baseline) or green (energized). Nickey Skarstad recolored her whole calendar this way. List the 2-3 moments in the last 12 months where you left with 2-4x the energy you entered with, and the biggest drains.
2. **Dig to cause, not label.** For each green and red, ask: why, and what was a specific good day versus bad day? Stop at the causal mechanism that transfers to other jobs, not 'I like the beach' (Moesta's three layers of language).
3. **Name your quest** (Moesta, from 1,000+ switch interviews): **Get out**, **Take the next step**, **Regain control**, **Realign to what you are good at**. Count pushes honestly; people leave when about 3-4 pushes stack up and a pull gives direction. If quest is 'get out' (burnout), a time-boxed low-demand 'jobcation' is legitimate.
4. **Translate features into experiences.** Title and money are static features; money usually stands for respect, bills or falling behind. Write the experiences you want (autonomy, a team that has your back, craft time).
5. **Three-factor filter for any option:** does it compound my learning, overlap my strengths, and will I have fun (Mayur Kamat)? Add Graham Weaver's check: when comparing A and B, notice which one has your energy; you may be talking yourself out of it.
6. **Done when:** you can write 'I do my best work when ___, I get drained by ___, and my next job must give me ___' in three lines.

### Play B: Career spec + The Skip (Deb Liu, Nikhyl Singhal)
1. **Write the spec.** End state in 5 years (not a title: what you build, with whom, what you can do), milestones, skills to acquire, and success metrics. Check every future decision against it (Deb Liu).
2. **Find the skip.** Define the job after the next one. Choose the next role for what it teaches toward that skip, not for the immediate title; stay in an ambiguous situation if it builds a needed skill (Singhal). Lateral escapes from a bad boss rarely help.
3. **Explore, then exploit** (Ada Chen Rekhi): early, treat each role as a hypothesis (try startup, growth, marketing) and stack complementary experiences; once something is rich, tell your manager what you want to learn and optimize for that over title.
4. **Write the skip-job story in the first person** for 6, 12 and 24 months: problem solved, skill built, headwind faced, how *I* overcame it. Use 'I', not 'we'. If the story is weak, change your work (Singhal).
5. **Run the learning loop** (Fareed Mosavat): execute on a real problem, generalize the lesson as a hypothesis, communicate it, then earn more scope and execute again. Revisit the spec yearly; Jason Shah warns that most moves rest on about 10 hours of signal, so hold plans loosely.
6. **Done when:** the spec is one page, the skip job has a name, and your current role has a written 'what this teaches me' line.

### Play C: Stay or leave (Bangaly Kaba, Nikhyl Singhal, Bob Moesta, Paige Costello)
1. **Score six environment variables 0-2 in quarter steps:** manager, resources, team, scope, compensation, culture (Kaba). For each low score ask: can it change, and will it?
2. **Fix before you flee.** Tell your manager dispassionately which variables hurt your output and ask for a plan; the manager is the variable that moves the others. If they will not or cannot, a new manager is the lever.
3. **Run the ex-growth test.** Still searching for product-market fit but valued in the hundreds of millions or more? You are at an ex-growth company; consider leaving (Singhal). Exceptions: the biggest role you can get, real bounded loyalty. Fear of finding another job is not a reason to stay.
4. **Check the learning curve** (Costello): is the steepness of my curve doing me a favor, is the environment healthy (staffing, advocates), is the problem interesting? Ada Chen Rekhi's frog test: is the temperature rising or falling on what I learn?
5. **Check the push-pull count.** Pushes alone make you a complainer (Moesta). Name the pull you are moving toward, and the 'lateral move' trap: leaving only for a bad boss or slow shipping is short-term (Singhal).
6. **Verdict:** Stay-and-fix (with a dated ask), Move internally (new manager or team), or Leave (with the pull named). Build the pros-and-cons spreadsheet, then 'put on the coat of the job': imagine the commute, the lunch, the problems of the day, and trust the gut reaction (Ami Vora, Tom Conrad).

### Play D: Promotion case (Singhal, Evans, Bavaro, Vo, Manik Gupta)
1. **Diagnose which of four causes** (Singhal): no advocate; the next role does not exist; impatience (leadership levels take longer than annual promotions); or a real development area you will not hear. Asking your manager alone often will not reveal it.
2. **Prerequisite:** be seen as solid in your current job, and 'good' is defined by your manager's view (Evans). Ask how you are doing and close any named gap first. Tamar Yehoshua: you will not get the next job unless you knock out the current one.
3. **Say the goal, framed in the future** (Jackie Bavaro): *I would like at some point to grow into [role]. What should I work on now so I am ready when it opens?* Then ask the manager's peers and people who have promoted others the same. Keep career talk a tiny share of 1:1s (Claire Vo).
4. **Run the Magic Loop** (Evans): do the job well; ask how you can help; do what they need, even unglamorous; then ask for work that serves them and moves you toward your goal; repeat. Early: ask how to help. Mid: suggest what needs doing. Senior: act and keep the leader informed, but only after trust is built. It works because managers help those who help them.
5. **Make yes easy.** Frame the promotion as the company's problem (span of control, unmet need), and when an org gap opens draw the org chart with your name in it, plus a JD and rollout (Claire Vo).
6. **Evidence in three parts** (Gupta): the *what* (end-to-end impact, rallying others behind a hypothesis), the *how* (creates clarity and energy), and followership (smart people choose to work with you). Add the seat test: a director has 6-8 reports, so check whether the role exists (Evans).
7. **Make impact legible.** Impact = work x your ability to show it, including teaching the org how your area works (Mosavat). Deb Liu: market your work like a product marketer; reframe the self-review as educating your manager.
8. **Done when:** you hold a one-page case (what, how, followership), a named sponsor, and a dated conversation with the decision maker.

### Play E: Job search system (Phyl Terry, Never Search Alone)
1. **Form a Job Search Council:** 6-8 peers, a volunteer moderator, a first trust-building meeting. Fast seekers (out of work) meet twice a week; slow seekers (employed) every two weeks. Terry's councils average a three-month search.
2. **Write the Mnookin two-pager:** what you want and do not want in the next role. A draft is fine; read others' lists to spot what you forgot.
3. **Run the listening tour** (three conversation types): reverse exit interviews with past colleagues (what was I good at, what am I a fit for, do I project it accurately); broader network using the golden question *If you were in my shoes, how would you approach this?*; and recruiters, asking what you are a fit for, not for a job. You are doing market research and creating invested 'listening posts'.
4. **Write the candidate market fit sentence:** 3-4 attributes (company stage, industry, level and function, culture) in one sentence, e.g. *director of product at a Series B healthcare startup in San Francisco*. Spear, not net; your council signs off, then you broadcast it. Expect your fit to be set by supply and demand (a director may fit senior-manager roles in a glut); consider a two-step path to a stretch target.
5. **Keep it alive:** send a monthly update to everyone you spoke with, even with no news; debrief right after every interview with a peer; keep recruiter relationships warm before you need them. Stalled searches stopped networking, left their council and never updated their fit.
6. **Done when:** you have the one-sentence fit signed off by your council, conversations booked in all three listening-tour types (Moesta suggests 10-15 informational interviews before you apply anywhere), and a council meeting rhythm.

### Play F: Interview to offer (Terry, Warwick, Nan Yu)
1. **Write a private job mission with OKRs** while interviewing: the outcomes you would be accountable for. After a couple of rounds, present it live (call, Zoom, coffee, never email) and ask what is and is not part of the role.
2. **Find the hiring manager's burning problem** (Nan Yu): ask their OKRs this quarter and how you could help. Ask for an intro to an engineering manager on the same problem.
3. **Before the offer:** do not name a number early; say you do not discuss compensation until you understand scope, then *what did you have in mind?* (Warwick). If the loop reveals a bigger role than the posted level, ask to restructure comp and level.
4. **At the offer: resources first, then money** (Terry). Agree OKRs with the hiring manager, name what you need to hit them (budget, headcount, tech-debt allowance, training or mentorship for juniors), ask whether they will commit, only then discuss comp. A company that reacts badly is a red flag.
5. **Ask for more, softly:** thank them, take a few days, then *What is the chance there could be a little more?* (Warwick). He sees about 20% improvement from simple pushback, around 40% with coaching. Terry reports most direct asks succeed. Never split the difference by reflex.
6. **Negotiate live with the hiring manager,** not by email through a recruiter. If stalled: *If you were in my shoes, what would you explore?* Check Adam Fishman's people-mission-financials criteria and back-channel references before signing.
7. **Done when:** you have the written agreed OKRs, a resources list, and a counter you can say in under three sentences.

### Play G: Stay current in the AI transition (Singhal, Andreessen, Acharya, Cat Wu)
1. **Cross the threshold:** decide that staying current outranks other priorities for the next two years (Singhal). Builders are wanted; PMs whose value was relaying information up the chain are most exposed.
2. **Ship one small thing a week** with an AI builder or agent (Anish Acharya); keep a silly 'chassis' project you iterate on with each new model. A full week solving a real problem end to end puts you in the top 1% of tool users by his estimate (Anton Osika).
3. **Retry what failed** with each model release (Ian Silber, Dan Shipper, Amol Avasare); do not marry your process, marry the outcomes you uniquely deliver (Andrew Ambrosino).
4. **Stack skills** (Andreessen): stay deep in your craft, add design/build/PM with AI as tutor; a job is a bundle of tasks, so list yours and swap the automatable ones for higher-level scope. Hand off the repetitive parts and use the freed time on work nobody had bandwidth for (Cat Wu).
5. **Own what you ship** (Zevi Arnovitz): 'AI built that' is never an excuse. Judgment and taste are the scarce layer.
6. **Done when:** you can show 3-5 shipped artifacts and a one-line answer to 'what do you build with agents?'

## Where the experts disagree
- **Plan it vs follow people.** Camp A: write a spec and work backward (Deb Liu, Nikhyl Singhal, Lauren Ipsen). Camp B: skip the five-year plan, take little bets and follow the best people (Tamar Yehoshua, Sanchan Saxena, Raaz Herzberg). *Use A when the destination is concrete (CPO, founder, board seat); B when you cannot yet say what you want or are under 5 years in.* Default: plan the skip loosely, run bets, review yearly.
- **Optimize for promotion vs optimize for impact.** Camp A: impact only, do not want it too badly (Shweta Shrivastava, John Mark Nickels, Chris Hutchins, Ian McAllister). Camp B: visibility and brand are part of the job (Deb Liu, Jeffrey Pfeffer, Laura Schaffer, Sriram Krishnan, Aarthi Ramamurthy). *Impact without legibility stalls; visibility without substance fails.* Default: impact first, then make it legible; at 50+ person orgs, budget for optics work (Doshi).
- **Generalist vs spike.** Camp A: refuse to specialize, fill the bingo card (Casey Winters, Shaun Clowes, Lauren Ipsen, Marc Andreessen). Camp B: double down on strengths, live on the edge of the triangle, get deep first (Jules Walter, Mayur Kamat, Hari Srinivasan, Tom Conrad, Noam Segal). *A for an executive path; B in the first 5 years and when you are burning out trying to be everything.* Default: one spike plus 1-2 adjacent stacks.
- **Learn to code in the AI era.** Camp A: yes, depth lets you steer and debug (Amjad Masad, Marc Andreessen, Scott Wu, Bret Taylor). Camp B: optimize for judgment and taste; titles collapse into builder (Boris Cherny, Lazar Jovanovic). *A if you want technical or infrastructure leverage; B is enough for most PMs.* Default: build weekly, learn enough to read and debug what the agent produces.

## Grade existing work
Score a career plan, promo doc, search plan or self-review 1/3/5 per criterion.
| Criterion | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Destination | A title or 'grow' | Role named, no why | Internally defined end state plus the skip job (Singhal, Norton) |
| Evidence of impact | Task lists, 'helped' | Outcomes without numbers | Numbers, 'I' language, others can cite it (LeMay, Ipsen) |
| Market test | Assumed | A few chats | Listening tour or leveling check with named fit statement (Terry) |
| Narrowness | Net: any PM role | Stage or industry only | One-sentence fit, 3-4 attributes (Terry) |
| Sponsor and network | Mentor only, or none | Mentors, no advocate | A named sponsor plus weak-tie pipeline (Mosavat, Pfeffer) |
| Manager alignment | Waiting to be noticed | Goal stated once | Future-framed ask, Magic Loop running (Bavaro, Evans) |
| Learning rate | Same work for 2 years | Some stretch | Recent 'oh shit' stretch moments, one skill at a time (Lane Shackleton, Vikrama Dhiman) |
| AI currency | No hands-on use | Uses chat tools | Ships weekly, owns outputs, can show artifacts (Singhal, Acharya) |
Output: score per criterion, total /40, and the **top 3 fixes** each naming the play and guest behind it.

## Red flags
- Asking how to get promoted before asking what the promotion is in service of (Singhal).
- Spray and pray after a layoff, skipping the deliberate 'what do I want' pause (Terry).
- Leaving for a bad boss or slow shipping alone, when the same problems exist next door (Singhal).
- Waiting for the manager to manage your career (Evans).
- Visibility without substance, or presentation polish over product outcomes (Pfeffer, Nickels).
- Logo collecting, or being loyal to a fault while the company tanks (Lauren Ipsen).
- Treating 'more money' as the reason; over half of those who said so did not get more (Moesta).
- Negotiating by email through a recruiter, or naming a number early (Warwick, Terry).
- Blaming engineering for missed deadlines in interviews (Ipsen); faking passion for a product (Deb Liu).
- Staying an information mover, or defending the old way of working that made you successful (Singhal's 'shadow superpower').
