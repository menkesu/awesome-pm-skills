# Frameworks: Customer Interviews

Every method below comes from Lenny's Podcast guests. Each entry: what it is for, when it applies or fails, steps, benchmark.

## 1. Interview method

**Switch interview / story extraction** (Bob Moesta, Bob Moesta episode) — Find out what made today the day someone bought. *Applies:* early research, repositioning, any product with a switching moment. *Fails:* purely habitual purchases. Steps: (1) frame the question: what causes today to be the day; (2) find people who recently purchased, yours or a competitor's; (3) extract the whole story: pushes, pulls, anxieties, habits, hire and fire criteria; (4) cluster stories into pathways of reasons that go together; (5) identify conflicting jobs and choose which to serve. Endorsed in spirit by Georgiana Laudi, Paul Adams, Robby Stein.

**Story-based interviewing** (Teresa Torres, Teresa Torres episode) — Opportunities come from specific past stories, not out-of-context questions. *Applies:* consumer and B2B discovery. Steps: ask for the last specific time they did the thing; have them set the scene; follow the timeline with 'what happened next?'; dig into friction as it surfaces; listen for unmet needs and desires, including ones they are unaware of. Echoed by Gokul Rajaram (ask for stories, not top problems) and Petra Wille (tell me about the last time).

**The one interview question** (Torres) — A full interview on one prompt: *Tell me about the last time you...* Then: set the scene, probe the trigger, *what did you do next?*, on friction ask how come and what you were looking at, summarize, return to the moment you want more of. Test of a good interview: it feels like a beer with a buddy.

**Forces of Progress** (Bob Moesta) — People move only when push away from the old (F1) plus pull of the new (F2) beats anxiety of the new (F3) plus habit of the present (F4). Adding features raises pull but also anxiety. Steps: find the push context; identify the pull; identify anxieties; identify habits; reduce anxiety and habit friction instead of only adding pull. *Fails:* no real choice or deep habit. Paul Adams: use the four forces simply and address anxieties directly when persuading.

**Layers of language** (Moesta) — Answers come as pablum, then fantasy or nightmare exaggeration, then what happened. Act like an investigator; reconstruct the real timeline, often longer than the buyer remembers.

**No discussion guide; bracket and play back wrong** (Moesta) — Use the forces as the frame, follow the most meaningful thread, say 'tell me more' or 'give me an example', bracket at the edge of language (more this or more that), and play the story back incorrectly so the person corrects you.

**Lightweight first JTBD** (Moesta) — Talk to 10 recent buyers about why they bought: what they hoped for, feared, gave up, how they convinced others. Note functional, emotional and social energy.

**Interview only people who tried to make the progress** (Moesta) — For zero-to-one and marketplaces, interview switchers of the alternatives (eBay, Etsy, Craigslist sellers) to derive jobs for a product that does not exist.

**Customer research question set** (Georgiana Laudi) — To best customers: what was going on when you looked for a solution, what was the trigger, what did you try and who influenced you, must-haves, anxieties, deal breakers, what can you do now that you could not. Best customers: paying, happy, low maintenance, signed up 3 to 6 months ago. Laudi notes surveys of recent best customers can be decisive without interviews.

**Big hire interrogation** (Robby Stein) — Ask why they use the product, where they were, what they were doing, until you reach the triggering moment.

**Feel the customer's bad feeling** (Nan Yu) — Keep asking from different angles until you reach the specific moment they felt bad and the emotional valence behind the request. Rapport unlocks the story. People will not answer 'how do you feel' directly early on.

**Art of inquiry** (Carole Robin) — Suspend judgment; ask what, when, where, how; avoid yes/no and why.

**Good question criteria** (Ada Chen Rekhi, from SurveyMonkey researchers) — A question is good if specific, solicits rationale, and is unbiased. Offer a constrained choice to lower burden.

**What questions should I have asked you?** (Christina Wodtke) — Closing question that knocks the person off script.

**Pings and echoes** (Matt Dixon) — For a stalling buyer, normalize a likely concern ('a lot of customers at this stage...') and listen for the echo. Executives consider themselves decisive, so direct questions about indecision offend.

**Don't pitch, sit in silence** (Jeff Weinstein) — Open with: *What's in your email right now?*, *What grinds your gears?*, *What did you do yesterday?* Then wait.

**Talk less in discovery** (Jeanne DeWitt Grosser) — Seller talks well under half the time; answer a question with a question about the question; five whys before solving.

**Happy ears / non-leading goal question** (Todd Jackson) — Ask top three goals for the quarter and what is in the way, then float a concept.

**Be vulnerable about being early** (Jen Abel) — Telling prospects you are early gets honest intel. Never ask what keeps you up at night; the answer changes daily. Ask the questions you are afraid to ask once a relationship exists.

**Wow statements and demonstrated behavior** (Todd Jackson) — Real interest is a wow reaction or a request for the deck or follow-up; 'interesting' is a polite no. Wes Kao: eyes lighting up is data. Christian Idiodi: ask for a reference to expose hesitation.

**Ability-to-pay questions / dollar-driven discovery** (Todd Jackson) — Extreme value, then ability to pay (already looking? built internally and failed? budget source, approval thresholds), then willingness to pay. Best customer: knows the problem and is already searching. Madhavan Ramanujam: never ask what to charge; use relative anchors, and ask why on both yes and no.

**Friend-zone talk** (Todd Jackson, Persona's Rick Song story) — First 5 to 10 customers: ask them not to be nice; is it a necessity, how painful if it vanished, would you switch to a competitor at half the price.

**Five whys for customer requests** (Yuhki Yamashita; Janna Bastow's Now-Next-Later story; Annie Pearl) — Ask why they want the feature, back up to the problem, ask why the problem exists. Build one scalable solution, not what customers A, B and C each ask.

**Ask what made you cancel** (Jason Cohen) — Open-ended, 'what made you cancel' doubled usable responses from 10 to 20 percent; randomized multiple-choice lists were noise.

**Observe, don't ask** (Gustaf Alstromer, Jag Duggal, Paige Costello) — Screen share, walk through the daily workflow, see workarounds. Ask indirect questions from several directions.

## 2. Who and how many

**Sample size rules** — Moesta: patterns repeat at 7 to 8, do 10 to 12, two rounds beat one of 24. Shaun Clowes: 7 to 14. Gustaf Alstromer: 25 to 50 to find an idea. Madhavan Ramanujam: B2B 20 to 30 accounts driving 80 to 90 percent of revenue; B2C 1,000 to 2,000 survey responses. Jag Duggal: call 10, by call five predict the rest. **Predictability stop:** 70 to 80 percent (Todd Jackson); you can predict the next answer (Nick Turley, whose general-purpose product never reached it early).

**Lighthouse customers** (Mike Maples Jr.; Tanguy Crusson; David Singleton's alpha group) — Customers living in the future; recruit about 10 you know by name, keep the whole team in contact over months, widen only once they are happy. Eventually the product must scale beyond them.

**Direct customer line / customers get write access** (Jeff Weinstein) — Text-message-friendly with 5 to 10 ambitious customers; invite them into meetings; have them draw the ideal dashboard. Friend feedback discounted to zero.

**Interview non-users and churners; funnel failures** (Mihika Kapoor, Chris Miller, Uri Levine) — Non-users reveal perception and onboarding gaps; interview those who dropped at each funnel stage. Users simply not a fit waste time.

**Economic buyer** (Geoffrey Moore) and **customer incentive analysis** (Jason Droege, April Dunford's champion secondary job: decide without getting fired).

**Find ICP via enthusiasts** (Oji Udezue) — After 100 or more users, look for people who really care and are not price sensitive.

**Deliberately ignore much early feedback** (Rahul Vohra; Jen Abel's 80/20) — Segment by whether the person values the core benefit.

## 3. Recruiting and cadence systems

**Automate interview recruiting** (Teresa Torres) — Choice architecture: in-product intercept, scheduler, sales or account managers recruit buyers, weekly trigger, recurring meeting. Floor: one interview per week.

**Automated pipeline from sales calls** (Kevin Yien) — Gong alerts to Slack to Zapier to email with Calendly; the sales team is a research team. Userinterviews.com for exact-ICP participants.

**In-journey recruiting and continuous conversations** (Oji Udezue) — Booking prompt inside activation or conversion; schedule conversations onto calendars weekly; friction kills discovery.

**Default-on customer contact** (Lane Shackleton) — Fixed weekly slot with something to show (Coda: Friday afternoons) beats an OKR to talk to 10 customers.

**Calendar test and whiteboard number** (Dalton Caldwell; Patrick Campbell) — 20 to 30 percent of founder time on customer meetings; ten non-sales conversations a month. Only one in five companies has personas or ICPs and one in ten does quarterly customer development.

**Weekly customer call email system** (Zoelle Egner) and **signup feed with one-click outreach** (Egner at Airtable) — Three hypothesis-selected users a week; phone over survey.

**Visit 30 customers** (Marty Cagan); **PM and designer attend every test**; **bring engineers** (Christine Itwaru); **demo sessions** (Guillermo Rauch); **live Slack thread** (Noah Weiss); **open customer-gap feed and email-emoji ritual** (Vijay Iyengar).

## 4. Synthesis

**Cluster into pathways** (Moesta) — Not themes, not segments.

**Customer job statement** (Georgiana Laudi) — *When I am in [situation], help me [motivation], so I can [outcome].* Jobs beat demographic personas for aligning marketing, product and CS (personas still help ad targeting).

**Context and outcome, not pain and gain** (Moesta) — Capture situation and desired end state; context makes irrational behavior rational. Struggling moments cause demand. Only people have jobs.

**Emotional vs utility jobs** (Robby Stein, Adam Fishman, Archie Abrams) — Most users arrive with an emotional frame; measure success on the emotional outcome.

**Opportunity space** (Teresa Torres) — Stack four skills: interview, hear opportunities, frame them specifically, pull a common structure across stories. Observation can beat interviews, but interviews sustain weekly.

**What, so what, then what** (Judd Antin) — Report the finding, the implication, then options; for bigger questions build an evidence-based framework and experiment together. For pointed micro problems the research can specify the fix.

**Say-do gap and stated preference traps** (Moesta Energy Star story; Kristen Berman; Merci Grace on invites; Bret Taylor on incorrect storytelling: stated reasons like 'too expensive' hide differentiation problems).

**Dining room table insight** (Moesta) — When behavior contradicts stated preference, dig for the emotional meaning and design for the hidden job (22 percent sales lift).

**Predictions before research** (Judd Antin on hindsight bias, citing Duncan Watts) — Write them down, compare.

**Effect size over p-value** (Noam Segal) — For large-sample survey findings use Cohen's d.

## 5. Choosing research and its altitude

**Macro, middle-range, micro** (Judd Antin) — Teams over-invest in middle-range. Frame each question against the funnel, strategy or OKRs; micro work (the seven-character button change, about 1 percent conversion) is high value. A researcher's full plate: two big projects and one small. Learn the business language: quarterly report, OKRs, funnel.

**We don't validate, we falsify** (Judd Antin) — Ask how we are wrong; bring the researcher before the decision.

**Research is scarce; research vs data by customer type** (Casey Winters) — Spend on problems big but not understood, or understood without a confident solution. Consumer scale: ship and measure; enterprise: talk to customers and sales; middle: pick the high-leverage areas.

**Don't talk to customers outside enterprise** (Keith Rabois) — Counterweight: subconscious purchase decisions; for enterprise with about 30 must-win accounts, talk to every decision maker.

**Hypothesis discipline** (Jag Duggal; Jake Knapp; Mihika Kapoor) — Crisp hypothesis, judge not lawyer, bring prototypes, enter with an A- idea.

**Customer listening and Feedback River** (Oji Udezue; Shaun Clowes via Sachin Rekhi) — Pipe all signals into one flow, triage, track topic frequency, cluster with LLMs. Vijay Iyengar: about 20 minutes a day at Mixpanel scale. Megan Cook: weekly all-company email of random feedback. Yuhki Yamashita: balanced feedback portfolio; Concerning Tweets channel.

**Findings repository and opt-in pool** (Melissa Perri) — Scale research without centralizing it; certified democratization (Fidelity).

**Quant to qual** (Laura Schaffer's anomaly to session replay; Jessica Lachs's calls after a failed test; Ravi Mehta's whale spenders) — Find the outlier in data, then call them.

**Immersion and empathy** (Peter Deng IDEO; Adriel Frederick and Kevin Aluwi doing the user's job; Maya Prohovnik feel the pain; Fiona Fung's Chile trip; Bangaly Kaba's on-the-ground trips; Tim Holley's seller visits) — Dogfooding warnings: Paige Costello, Alexander Embiricos, Upasna Gautam (you are only a proxy).

**Working sessions** (Upasna Gautam) — Rename user testing; users recreate workflows; 3 to 6 two-hour sessions before onboarding each team.

**Don't blindly follow customers in AI** (Sherwin Wu) — Customers anchor on local maxima; weigh requests against model trajectory over 1 to 2 years. Simon Willison: real humans for usability. Tomer Cohen's persona research agent and Dan Shipper's agent over a folder of interviews are aids, not replacements (Peter Deng).
