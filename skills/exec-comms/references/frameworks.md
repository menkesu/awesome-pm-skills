# Exec Comms: Frameworks

Grouped by theme. Credit is to the originator named in the digest; other endorsers are noted.

## 1. Structuring written messages

**Sales, then logistics** (Wes Kao, Wes Kao 2.0) — For asking people to do something new (fill in a form, join a ritual). Applies to peers and execs. Steps: open with why we are doing this, why it matters to the company, why now; frame it as a benefit to the audience; only after buy-in share doc, deadline, format.

**TLDR then context** (Wes Kao) — Gist or decision at the top, `Context:` beneath, possibly several paragraphs. Readers who agree stop. Fails or reads as aggressive in external or relationship-sensitive messages (Lenny's note). Endorsed by Ian McAllister (answer first) and Maggie Crowley (Minto).

**Answer first, then explain** (Ian McAllister) — Answer a when question with a date, a how much with a number; then background only if needed. Avoid weasel words; own your problems. Self-grade after every doc, meeting or hallway answer.

**Minto principle plus three-or-fewer** (Maggie Crowley) — Lead every email and doc with the conclusion; cap strategy bets, conclusions and next steps at three; delete the first one or two paragraphs you wrote as warm-up. Jules Walter credits Minto's Pyramid Principle as the first comms skill to learn.

**Recommendation first, then pros and cons** (Wes Kao) — A pros and cons list implies objectivity, so a skewed one destroys trust. State the recommendation, list honest pros, cons and risks.

**Separate FYI, background and the ask** (Wes Kao) — Do not interweave action items with context. Make the split explicit and say what you want the reader to chime in on. Ebi Atawodi's version: say whether the email is an FYI, a problem or a request for help.

**Flashtags** (Dharmesh Shah) — Escalating tags so recipients know the weight of your input: #FYI (no response), #suggestion (I would consider it), #recommendation (researched; if you decline, say why), #plea (deeply held, still not a mandate). Search the tags later to see how past recommendations turned out. Works best in async and email cultures; for founders whose offhand opinions get treated as orders.

**Intent tags on leadership messages** (Brian Halligan) — Tag each note: get this done this week, let's discuss, or FYI-I'm-thinking. Be repetitive on core messages; hallway ideas get built by someone.

**Quick frame at the start of every conversation** (Wes Kao) — One to two minutes: why we are talking, the problem it solves, exactly what you need (feedback and agreement). Prevents cold starts with busy stakeholders.

**Signposting** (Wes Kao) — Use words that guide: because, for example, as a next step, first, second, third. Verbal version for demos: the most important part to pay attention to is, the part that surprised us most is.

**Full sentences, sparse formatting** (Wes Kao, contrarian) — Turning each bullet fragment into a full sentence tests whether the idea is finished and shows the logic between claims. If everything is bold, nothing is highlighted. Opposed by the common bullet-and-bold advice. Naomi Gleit adds: use numbered lists, never bullets, so people can refer to an item.

**Speak accurately** (Wes Kao) — Match stated conviction to the evidence in both directions. Frame hunches as my initial thinking is; share a point of view, then back it with evidence; do not undercut a well-founded recommendation. Important when the ask consumes headcount or money.

**Accidental leading questions** (Wes Kao) — A sentence carries a baked-in point of view. Either neutralize it or state your recommendation on purpose.

**Concise means density** (Wes Kao) — Economy of words, not word count. A tight 1,000-word memo beats a meandering 300-word one. Re-reading before sending usually trims 20% and moves secondary points to a thread. Julian Shapiro adds cut two-thirds of a draft to find the core.

**Concrete writing rules** (Jason Shah, Amazon) — Numbers over adjectives; never write vague words like great; cut relentlessly; check the strategic implication of each specific claim. Shah warns that moving from slides to docs fails when people import slide mindsets and just add words.

**Amazon Working Backwards PRFAQ** (Jason Shah; also Bill Carr in the spec skill) — Write the press release (announcement, problem, solution, customer quote, leadership quote, call to action) plus external and internal FAQs before building. Fails as a substitute for iterating the real product.

**Read it out loud / just say that** (Maggie Crowley; Emilie Gerber's jargon test) — Read aloud; if it sounds complicated, ask what you are trying to say and write exactly that. Gerber: define every buzzword, answer what does it do, who is it for.

**Dummy explanation** (Raaz Herzberg) — Assume the reader knows nothing about your company, product or market; drop internal terms; resist changing a message just because you are sick of it. Petra Wille adds: drop business lingo for sensory, human words.

**Nail the first three sentences** (Keith Rabois) — The framing at the top took the litigator a week; the rest took two days. With AI, the human still owns the opening.

**Cadence interruption** (Kevin Yien) — Vary sentence length on purpose; monotonous cadence makes readers tune out.

**Canonical nomenclature** (Naomi Gleit) — Write the shared vocabulary with definitions (for example consistency vs accuracy) so functions stop talking past each other.

## 2. Docs that carry strategy

**Two-page strategy doc** (Ebi Atawodi) — Insights, approach, big rocks; circulate to partners and engineering; build the roadmap from it. Clarity comes from writing.

**Strategy document template** (Chandra Janakiraman) — Leader context, key insights, strategic pillars with the why, bold winning aspiration with illustrative concepts, alignment questions, appendix with the full scoring table. Three to four pages plus appendix; no roadmap inside.

**Evergreen team narrative doc** (Ebi Atawodi) — One doc with a memorable short link on why the team exists, problems, and deliverables; send it with intro requests and onboarding; refresh quarterly. Many calls fall off.

**Write the doc you keep explaining** (Alex Komoroske; Lane Shackleton echoes) — If the same explanation worked across ten one-on-ones, spend 30 minutes making it a document.

**Themes for discussion** (Jessica Fain) — When an exec leaves 100 comments, bubble the controversial themes to the top; answer factual items offline; confirm you read their reaction correctly.

**Present multiple options** (Jessica Fain) — One option invites did you miss something. Show a few with your recommendation argued; keep other explored options in an appendix.

**Match the exec's preferred format** (Jessica Fain) — Execs differ on docs vs decks, data vs story, pre-reads vs live reading. Ask, observe, offer 10 minutes of silent reading; if you are the exec, say yours.

**Slide doc** (Nancy Duarte, contrarian) — A deck built to be read without a presenter: full sentences, strong pictures, five front slides, long appendix. She argues it beats the six-page memo for circulating ideas.

**Structured narrative instead of slides** (Ronny Kohavi) — A document of questions each idea must answer, reviewed in a commentable doc, so feedback stays attached. Tamar Yehoshua adds the six-pager rule: the leader speaks last.

**HPM: Highlights, People, Me** (Boz Bosworth) — Weekly manager report: big items leadership needs, people risks and wins, how I am doing. Ask each manager how they like updates.

**Three things this week and ahead** (Evan Spiegel) — Leaders send weekly three from this week and three looking ahead; pair with an agent over dashboards for the CEO's situational awareness.

**Function deep dives** (Casey Winters) — Each exec writes where their function is, issues, and a 3 to 5 year direction; forces explanation and builds shared understanding.

**Show six months of valuable work** (Will Larson) — A roadmap of meaty shipped work with its impact builds trust with CEOs and boards.

**Portfolio roadmap for execs** (Melissa Perri) — Execs want big pushes tied to strategy and R&D allocation, not Jira tickets.

**Document decisions with assumptions** (Megan Cook) — Record assumptions and hypotheses so a year later you can see why.

**Collective-time rule** (Tara Seshan) — Invest at least the collective reading or meeting time in prep.

## 3. Exec meetings and reviews

**Prepared for the whole universe of questions** (Casey Winters) — Predict every question the document could prompt, check you can answer each with data, role-play each exec, hold pre-meetings. Three to dozens of hours. Avoid the big-reveal aha format. Executive communication is communication with individual executives.

**Start at the right chapter** (Casey Winters) — Start from the last point that is obvious to this exec (strategy, metrics, assumptions), then step into the less obvious. Needs tuning per audience.

**Dinosaur brain** (Ami Vora) — Assume an exec holds about three facts; do the deep analysis and bring a recommendation; let the exec contribute breadth. Daniel Lereya: give the three most meaningful items, not everything.

**30-second meeting opener** (Jessica Fain) — What we are here to discuss; where we left off; goals; how we will run it; stop; ask if there is anything else they hoped to cover.

**Setting up the decision meeting** (Megan Cook) — State what you need (decision, feedback, hypothesis test); brief narrative of situation, what changed and implications; a few key data points; know the underlying data.

**Yes, yes, yes, next, next, next** (Wes Kao) — Execs listen in got it, keep going mode; if they agree early, take the win and skip slides.

**Play like it is game day** (Wes Kao) — Practice exec-level clarity with peers, managers and reports; you cannot get enough reps with execs alone.

**Answer the question they should have asked** (Paige Costello, contrarian) and **Question behind the question** (Wes Kao, contrarian) — Listen for the real concern; answer the bigger issue or missed alternative; offer adjacent info you do have; never fabricate. Wes Kao notes less experienced people may be better saying they will follow up.

**Convey confidence through openness** (Paige Costello) — Stay present, scan faces for questions, close by asking what you missed.

**Anchor on the user** (David Singleton) — If you understand the user, off-script questions are easy to bring back.

**Limit the deck for combustible founders** (Chip Conley) — Short deck, used only to set principles and goals.

**Reaction-speed baseline** (Wes Kao) — Measure by the touch points and friction needed to get the reaction you want; try one tactic at a time.

**Blast radius of a memo** (Wes Kao) — A confusing message to a channel multiplies into back-and-forth; 30 seconds of cleanup unblocks everyone.

## 4. Persuasion, narrative and decks

**Audience is the hero** (Nancy Duarte) — The audience holds the power; you are the mentor who helps them get unstuck. Ask who they are and what is hard about your ask; frame the call to action as help.

**What is / What could be / New bliss** (Nancy Duarte; John Mark Nickels and Mike Maples Jr. teach the same toggle) — State current reality, move to the better future, alternate several times, end on the new bliss. Works in a conversation too. Duarte notes conclusion-first suits execs and fundraising; skeptical audiences need the three-act build.

**Big idea and one point per slide** (Nancy Duarte) — Audience journey (from where to where), big idea (point of view plus stakes), only slides that support it, one point each.

**Three-bullet checklist** (Nancy Duarte) — Audience is the hero; infuse story; can they see what I am saying?

**Star moment** (Nancy Duarte) — One memorable item: dramatic number, evocative story or striking picture.

**Sketch storyboard before software** (Nancy Duarte, Maggie Crowley, Janna Bastow) — Boxes on paper, headline per box, then choose slide types. Crowley says a colleague cut deck time by 75%.

**Make ideas visible** (Nancy Duarte) — Say what you see, draw it, ask if it matches, co-create until everyone sees the same steps. Leader sees the mosaic; team sees tiles.

**Pixar process for high stakes** (Nancy Duarte) — Narrative and big idea, script chunks, models developed in parallel with stakeholders, then reassemble. Match slide density to audience culture; dense team slides are fine internally; take slides away at emotional peaks.

**Slide title is the takeaway** (Andy Raskin; de Montebello's one thing per slide agrees) — Replace labels with takeaway sentences.

**Pain point, solution, proof point** (Mihika Kapoor) — Repeat the cycle with prototypes and user testimonials rather than listing all pains then all solutions.

**Vision pitch deck** (Melanie Perkins) — Put vision in a deck so others see and react to your thinking.

**Show every side of the cathedral** (Lane Shackleton) — Couple the strategy write-up with a metric and directional mocks.

**Start the pitch in the middle of the action** (Merci Grace, contrarian) — Open with your unique insight, not the market; backfill after you have attention.

**Hook, middle, success ending** (Christina Wodtke) — Hook with mystery or the conclusion, deliver the message in the middle, end with success; get feedback from a trusted listener. Stories carry facts: people forget lists but remember a story full of facts.

**Hero's journey for team stories** (Petra Wille) — Choose the hero (team to motivate, user to show life improved), name the demons, define the call and the brighter future. Story in three lengths (75 seconds, 6 minutes, 80 minutes) and three forms (spoken, written, visual). Invest about two weeks at one to two hours a day for a 3 to 4 month strategy story; skip for a sprint.

**Four ways to keep people listening** (Matthew Dicks) — Stakes, surprise, suspense, humor; audit each section for at least one. Five stakes devices: elephant, backpack, breadcrumbs, hourglass, crystal ball. Keep adding stakes, with most in the first half. Start with location and action; apply the dinner test; nostalgia can prove domain expertise.

**Humor mechanics** (Dharmesh Shah, Matthew Dicks) — Laughs per minute: above about 1.0 to 1.25 puts a non-professional in the top decile. Punchline last, then stop; stack punchlines on one setup; two expected, one unexpected.

**State change method** (Wes Kao) — Every 3 to 5 minutes or slides, shake the audience awake: chat prompts, polls, guesses, breakouts, switching speaker or view. Guess before you reveal.

**Memification** (Yuhki Yamashita) — Distill an insight until execs repeat it unprompted; reset your internal computer to explain it like someone with no context. Synthesis of many opinions is the PM's storytelling power.

**Leave one dot unconnected** (Alex Komoroske) — Use an evocative metaphor that connects nine of ten dots so the audience connects the last, useful for uncomfortable truths.

**Narrative tension in strategy** (Richard Rumelt) — Frame a real choice (left or right) to catch emotional interest.

## 5. Live speaking and Q&A

**What? So what? Now what?** (Matt Abrahams) — For updates, feature descriptions, standups, feedback.

**PREP** (Matt Abrahams) — Point, reason, example, point restated.

**ADD: Answer, Detailed example, Describe relevance** (Matt Abrahams) — Rewrite company FAQs this way. Numeric question: just give the number.

**Problem, Solution, Benefit** (Matt Abrahams) — Advertising structure for pitches and explanations.

**WHAT toast** (Matt Abrahams) — Why are we here, How connected, Anecdote, Thanks. Show emotion through anecdote.

**Make it a conversation / distract the audience** (Matt Abrahams) — Open with the three questions you will answer, or a short video, question or story, so you facilitate rather than perform. Distractions must be relevant.

**Minimally viable communication** (Matt Abrahams) — Draft, test with a few people, iterate; Abrahams adds prepare to be spontaneous and dare to be dull. Q&A pet peeves: good question for everything, does that make sense.

**Strong direction, think up, speak before you think** (Tristan de Montebello) — Choose a clear point in sentence one; look up when you pause; shorten the pause before speaking.

**Summary prompts and End strong** (Tristan de Montebello) — Use stock closers and avoid hedges as doubt creeps in near the end; audiences remember the ending.

**Stay in character** (Tristan de Montebello) — Do not announce doubts; audiences cannot see them. Real factual errors may need correction.

**Conviction prompts** (Tristan de Montebello) — Practice sentence starters like we cannot forget that. Apply to the roughly 90% who under-commit; the other 10% need to question themselves. Pfeffer's appearing powerful checklist: no notes, eye contact, no apologies, gestures, louder, humor.

**Be interested, not interesting; reciprocal disclosure; supporting vs shifting responses** (Matt Abrahams) — Small talk mechanics: ask more than you tell, match disclosure depth.

## 6. Preparing and rehearsing a talk

**Accordion Method** (Tristan de Montebello) — Speak the talk at 3, 2, 1, 0.5 minutes, cut noise each time, then expand back to 1, 2, 3 keeping intentional pieces. Write only the one thing plus three bookmarks. About 15 minutes; one or two refreshers before the talk.

**Bow and Arrow** (Tristan de Montebello) — Choose the one sentence you want remembered; add weight with stories, data, emotion; ask whether you would be satisfied if that were all they recalled.

**Record, listen passively, rehearse transitions** (Matthew Dicks) — People forget transitions, not content; do not memorize verbatim. Paul Adams froze after memorizing for the first time.

**Rehearse 10 times with final notes** (Maya Prohovnik) — Find your note style, rehearse the final version ten full times, ad-lib half the notes, make eye contact.

**Narrative before slides** (Janna Bastow) — Write the story first, fit slides to it, record yourself and listen back.

**Hone the story by retelling** (Tony Fadell on Steve Jobs) — Pitch smart outsiders daily and refine; 10,000 to 100,000 reps. Petra Wille: ask a trusted peer (harsher) and strangers (spot gaps).

**Practice games** (Tristan de Montebello) — Conductor (energy 1 to 10), Triple Step (one direction while absorbing random words), warm-up reps before interviews. Practice in turbulence; short reps with feedback.

**55 of 60 minutes on content** (Sean Ellis) — Confidence comes from well-organized content; spend most prep on structure, little on practice. Contrast with Tristan's rehearsal-heavy method.

## 7. Nerves and presence

**Rule of lung** (Matt Abrahams) — Exhale twice as long as the inhale; relaxation happens on the exhale.

**Reframe anxiety** (Matt Abrahams, Maya Prohovnik) — Excitement or adrenaline; same arousal.

**Visualization** (Matt Abrahams; Nancy Duarte's friendly face) — See the room, getting up, being well received, stepping off; sit in the audience seats and picture the friendly face.

**Personal mantra, tongue twisters, embodied cognition** (Matt Abrahams) — Short mantra scheduled before speaking; warm the voice; change the body to change the mind.

**Normalize anxiety** (Matt Abrahams, Matthew Dicks) — Most people peak one minute before and during the first minute; roughly 98% of nerves come before you begin.

**Pre-talk ritual** (Nancy Duarte) — Darkness, slow breathing, a saved funny clip; test calm vs pump-up. Janna Bastow: power pose, walk the empty stage at tech check, speak to nodding faces.

**Start small and friendly** (Petra Wille, Julie Zhuo) — Team, all-hands, local meetups; or a 52-week publishing habit.

## 8. Communicating as a leader and in change

**Communication is the job** (Boz Bosworth) — If it did not land, that is your failure; silence is communication; address fears and the premise first; repeat across modalities; explain the delta between perception and reality.

**Repeat across channels** (Claire Hughes Johnson, Molly Graham) — No single email or all-hands reaches everyone; people did not hear you the first 43 times.

**Be the custodian of the message** (Jeetu Patel, contrarian) — Tell the story yourself; layers degrade it like packet loss. Fails under quiet-period limits.

**Transparency over process** (Hilary Gridley) — Ten approvers on an email is a transparency problem; teach how leaders think.

**Honest uncertainty template** (Dr. Becky Kennedy) — Acknowledge you heard everything, be honest about conviction, state the direction, set a reassessment date, rally.

**Under-communicating upward** (Casey Winters) — Escalate problems and the explicit trade-offs so execs can help and fairly evaluate. Alex Komoroske: status rollups compound small lies.

**Talk when you are in the right** (Matt Mullenweg) — In public disputes, long-form real-time engagement is more credible than lawyers; watch legal exposure.

## 9. Writing habits and AI

**Writing as thinking vs reporting** (Tara Seshan) — Automate reporting, never thinking; start yourself, end yourself, use AI in the middle for research and pushback.

**AI slop test** (Dan Shipper) — Fine if you stand behind every line; slop if it took less time to write than to read.

**Question-first writing** (Geoff Charles) — Write the question at the top, close the laptop, answer it yourself first.

**Habits** (Julie Zhuo, Will Larson, Brandon Chu, Lenny Rachitsky) — Word-count goals not time goals; publish almost everything; write what energizes you and relates to your work; a quality post is about 40 hours (2 drafting, 38 editing and feedback); iterate about 50 passes; share raw drafts early.

**Shop the brief and invite attack** (Tara Seshan) — Write deeply, then have people poke holes, repeat with the next person.

**Style extraction** (Dan Shipper) — Have an agent read a corpus, extract the technique, write itself a style guide.

**Researcher agent for meeting prep** (Aparna Chennapragada) — Give an agent the meeting goal and attendees; ask for each person's views and a persuasion pitch.

**Writing quality = novelty x resonance** (Julian Shapiro) — Draft one finds novelty; draft two adds stories, analogies, examples.
