# Growth Model: Frameworks

Every distinct framework from the digest, grouped by theme. Each entry: originator and episode, what it is for, when it applies or fails, steps, benchmarks, and who else endorses it.

## A. Sequencing, strategy and measurement

### 1. Kindle and fire strategies (Casey Winters, Casey Winters episode)
- **For:** deciding when to stop hacking and hire for scale.
- **Applies:** early-stage companies deciding when to hire growth people.
- **Steps:** (1) Use founder-led, non-scalable hacks (kindle) to get first users. (2) Use them to unlock one scalable fire strategy: content loop, sales loop, viral loop or paid. (3) Once a fire strategy is unlocked, hire a full-time owner (salesperson, growth PM, performance marketer) to make it 10x better.
- **Endorsed by:** Gustaf Alströmer (doing things that don't scale is not a scalable strategy; eventually a specific channel must emerge), Dalton Caldwell (study comps' first thousand users, not what they do today).

### 2. Acquisition loop as a requirement for PMF (Casey Winters)
- **For:** pre-PMF founders designing the product.
- **Claim:** a product that retains but has no scalable loop to find more users does not truly have PMF. Build loops into the product before PMF so distribution is ready when PMF arrives, without scaling prematurely.
- **Endorsed by:** Ben Williams (hold an explicit hypothesis for where new users come from), Seth Godin (will this work better for users if they tell others), Evan Spiegel and Mark Pincus (distribution is a first-class problem, hope is not a strategy).

### 3. Growth sequencing: activation first, acquisition last (Sean Ellis)
- **For:** ordering work after a PMF signal.
- **Steps:** activation (great first experience) → engagement and referral → revenue model → then scale acquisition channels; run only enough acquisition to keep flow-through before that.
- **Why:** acquisition is too competitive to scale without efficient convert, retain and monetize.
- **Endorsed by:** Elena Verna (never start with product-led acquisition), Bangaly Kaba (leaky bucket makes top-of-funnel size irrelevant), Jag Duggal (viral loops need no leak).

### 4. Value delivery engine / growth process (Sean Ellis)
- **For:** a first growth operating system.
- **Steps:** understand the must-have value (PMF survey) → pick a metric capturing units of value delivered, decided by the team → diagram the engine as it exists (onboarding, aha, engagement loop, referral) → find highest-leverage improvements → run experiments.
- **Fails:** hard to retrofit in late-stage companies.

### 5. Understand, identify, execute (Naomi Gleit; Jules Walter)
- **For:** teams without trustworthy funnel data.
- **Steps:** instrument every registration and onboarding step → understand where users drop (Facebook: 20% lost at email confirmation) → identify the biggest opportunities → build product changes.
- **Story:** Facebook's early growth team stopped roadmap work for a period to instrument. Jules Walter used the same loop at Slack, moving activation double digits in about six months by spending most time on why people do not stay.
- **Also:** Bangaly Kaba at Instagram (first quarter was logging an eight-step sign-up funnel), Kayvon Beykpour (instrumenting found an SMS verification bug).

### 6. Growth accounting (Naomi Gleit)
- **For:** choosing between acquisition and retention.
- **Steps:** define stale as no login for 30 days and resurrected as returning after 30 days → net growth per day = new registrations − stale + resurrected → compare line sizes → shift effort to the largest (at Facebook, churn and resurrection lines were larger than new users).
- **Applies:** consumer or login-based engagement with meaningful churn.

### 7. Churn ceiling (Jason Cohen)
- **For:** making churn visceral.
- **Formula:** max customers = new customers per month ÷ monthly logo cancellation rate. 100 new per month at 5% churn caps at 2,000.
- **Insight:** cancellations grow automatically with the base; marketing does not. Do it in dollars for revenue churn too.
- **Companion:** Dan Hockenmaier, building a growth model showed growth is far more sensitive to retention than intuition suggests (Thumbtack shifted resources from top-of-funnel to lifecycle).

### 8. Growth physics and levers (Crystal Widjaja)
- **For:** a quantitative model that fits real constraints.
- **Steps:** (1) list market, consumers and supply, product, monetization and channels, including real-world channels; (2) fit loops and funnels with quantitative inputs inside those constraints; change one small parameter at a time. Start from what works and the biggest constraint.
- **Also from her:** the sandbox constraint (narrow a vague goal like retention to the step right before the decision), the familiarity loop (tie an unfamiliar product to a familiar object).

### 9. Growth team phases, staffing and first hire (Shaun Clowes, Melissa Tan, Merci Grace, Adam Fishman, Peter Deng, Emily Kramer)
- **Phases (Clowes):** prove any growth effect exists → make it a repeatable system → scale a PLG lens across acquisition to expansion → fit with product, sales and marketing. Many fail by chasing random unexplainable wins.
- **First hire (Tan):** an acquisition portfolio manager who tests one or two hypothesized channels (usually an 80/20 split), checks signups are quality and monetizable. Start growth once rough PMF exists: people still use it six months later, get it after 20 minutes of white-glove explanation, and pay (Merci Grace).
- **Competencies (Fishman):** growth loop modeling, capital allocation and forecasting, prioritization and roadmapping.
- **Contrarian (Deng):** hire a growth leader before an analytics team; growth leaders tied to outcomes force instrumentation.
- **Split (Kramer):** marketing owns inbound and web conversion; a growth PM owns in-product experiments; pair them on onboarding and sign-up with a DRI.
- **Warning (Chris Miller):** the number one mistake is hiring a head of growth with no tooling, engineering, design or data and a big number.

### 10. Overlay growth models (Elena Verna, Verna 3.0)
- **For:** avoiding S-curve decline.
- **Steps:** optimize the current model → overlay another (PLG, marketing, sales) before the slowdown → expect most loops to spin out in 5-7 years → introduce something new about every 18 months and a major engine about every 5 years.
- **Timing:** new loops take 6-18 months for visible revenue.
- **Counterpoints:** Dropbox sharing lasted 17 years; Jason Cohen's elephant curve (channels rise, plateau, then sag as audiences saturate and others copy).

### 11. Portfolio sizing: lead bullets, cannonballs and big swings (Adriel Frederick, Amol Avasare, Brian Chesky, Deb Liu)
- **Lead bullets and cannonballs (Frederick):** no silver bullets; many small tweaks plus occasional cannonballs (phone-number sign-up, friend recommendations).
- **AI-first flip (Avasare):** traditional growth is 60-70% small-medium bets; for AI-first products 50-70% larger swings because product value may be 100-1000x higher later.
- **80/20 passes (Chesky):** 80% big leaps, 20% running the ball, the reverse of most companies.
- **Hundred-hypothesis batches (Deb Liu):** keep about 100 hypotheses, run 10 per sprint cycle. Growth is a game of inches: 1-5% improvements compound.
- **Choose by context:** small bets in stable scaled businesses, big swings in new fast categories (see SKILL.md debates).

### 12. Reinvention over optimization (Elena Verna, Verna 4.0)
- **For:** new, crowded categories (vibe coding). About 95% innovation, 5% optimization at Lovable; only 30-40% of her prior playbook transferred.
- **Steps:** stop optimizing existing journeys, stand up new loops and features one after another, capture demand before competitors. Growth team ships core product (integrations, voice mode) when it lifts engagement.
- **Fails:** scaled stable businesses where optimization still matters.

## B. Activation and onboarding

### 13. Finding the aha moment: correlation then experiment (Hila Qu)
- **Steps:** brainstorm high-value actions → correlation of each against both conversion and retention versus average → shortlist, combining if none stands out → run experiments to push users to the action and check conversion and retention rise.
- **Fails:** correlation alone gives false aha moments. Examples: Acorns recurring investment predicted retention; GitLab two users and two features in 14 days.

### 14. Choosing an activation metric (Sean Ellis; Ben Williams; Lauryn Isford)
- **Ellis:** start qualitatively, shortlist 2-3 behaviors, check correlation with long-term retention, prove causation, prefer behaviors reachable in the first session over late ones like used it 100 times.
- **Williams (Snyk):** activation is a team habit of deriving core value (fixing vulnerabilities within 30 days of team creation); work back to aha moment, setup moment and steps.
- **Isford:** prefer a specific, lower-rate definition (5-15% of users) that tracks retention; aim to move true activators from 5% to 6-7%.
- **Dan Hockenmaier's caution:** best users do X is not a strategy; something unique about them drives it.

### 15. Escape velocity thresholds (Sri Batchu; Naomi Gleit; Merci Grace; Oji Udezue)
- **For:** setting a numeric target.
- **Examples:** Facebook 7 friends in 10 days or 10 in 14; Instacart 3 orders in first month; Ramp 4 events in 30 days; Slack 3 real people and 50 real messages (regression then product hypothesis tests); Twitter follow graph (Udezue); Calendly first meeting then five in a week; Duolingo seven-day streak (section C).
- **Three thresholds (Udezue):** define three increasing thresholds and optimize the drop-off between each.

### 16. Value milestone map: struggle, evaluation, growth (Georgiana Laudi)
- **Steps:** struggle (problem, interest) → evaluation (first value, value realization) → growth (frequency or habit, expansion). About six milestones. Ask what convinced customers the product would work to define first value. Build a proactive onboarding map plus a win-back map.
- **Example:** SparkToro first value was 5+ searches plus a list; trial-to-paid doubled.
- **Contrarian:** Laudi says forget the funnel; funnels and MQLs center the business, not the customer. Roughly 70% of people log in once and never return.

### 17. Join, Evaluate, Upgrade, Expand (Lauryn Isford)
- **For:** communicating where investments sit. Evaluate is deliberately not onboard or activate: what the user needs to see to believe they will get value and build a habit. Expansion drives net dollar retention, brand awareness and renewals.

### 18. Portfolio onboarding rebuild (Lauryn Isford)
- **Bets:** guided onboarding wizard (step-by-step questions with buttons, workflow rendered live beside them), use-case personalization, ongoing education; retire tooltips.
- **Result:** +20% activation over 6-8 months. Ship one generic flow serving 90%+ first, then personalize; segment by learning and building style; ask collaborator versus builder at sign-up.
- **Rules:** meet users where they are, separate necessary from ongoing education, do not name features, do not map onboarding to pricing.
- **Fails:** simple products where scaffolding is not the barrier.

### 19. First mile: lazy, vain and selfish (Scott Belsky; Anuj Rathi; Grant Lee)
- **Claim:** in the first 30 seconds users want speed, to look good and to feel successful, with no tour. Each new cohort needs a new onboarding because later pragmatists are less forgiving.
- **Grant Lee:** throw one egg (one compelling first experience); Gamma's rebuild of the first 30 seconds took signups from hundreds to 20,000+ a day. Create and share, dead simple.
- **Anuj Rathi:** assume they have heard of you 20 times; continue the marketing message into the splash and onboarding.

### 20. Mandatory versus optional onboarding (Oji Udezue)
- **Steps:** mandatory setup of at most three screens (what it does plus essential setup; Calendly: connect calendar, set default hours); everything else optional and random-access; point to the one thing to do next; show one or two examples of good; make trial status explicit. Wizards had limited benefit. Onboarding substitutes for sales and account management at scale.

### 21. The right friction (Elena Verna, Laura Schaffer, Kristen Berman, Amol Avasare, Nickey Skarstad, Adam Fishman)
- **Claim:** friction that reassures users they are in the right place raises activation; pointless friction hurts.
- **Evidence:** Twilio four dropdowns +5% conversion with no personalization; Trunk Club +133% from benefit questions; TytoCare quiz completers purchased 53% vs 37%; Etsy added friction so sellers reached a first sale faster; Verna: profile users at sign-up with three to four screens (company size, department, use case, seniority); Fishman: good onboarding can lower conversion if retention rises.
- **Fails:** hard open-text questions (an open-text removal improved conversion 40%); untested flows. Split dense screens into two (Avasare).
- **Counter-camp:** Belsky, Bier, Schillace (adoption effort must be a fraction of payoff), Archie Abrams (lowering top-of-funnel friction hurts rates but lowers CAC more).

### 22. Pill in the hot dog (Laura Schaffer)
- **Steps:** interview users to find the scary or unfamiliar step; remove it from the lead position; start in a familiar context (docs, code samples); embed the scary step later. Quick Deploy (no-code demo) for non-developers generated tens of millions in pipeline.

### 23. Three Bs and behavioral diagnosis (Kristen Berman)
- **Three Bs:** pick an uncomfortably specific behavior (two 10-minute workouts with two instructors within 7 days, never log in); reduce logistical and cognitive barriers; add immediate benefits for present bias.
- **Behavioral diagnosis:** screenshot every step to the behavior (often 200-300 slides), overlay the psychological barrier, brainstorm interventions. Align the team by asking each member separately what engagement means.
- **Related:** defaults and rules of thumb, deadlines are a gift, right for wrong, friction to reduce a behavior.

### 24. Do, show, tell and warm starts (Hila Qu; Merci Grace)
- Remove friction and give a warm start with sample data or templates; email brings dropped users back. Teach the product with the product (a to-do that tells you to complete it). Design onboarding from day one by mapping steps between the user and full value. Do not adopt plug-and-play onboarding frameworks or copy Slack's flow.

### 25. Human-first, then productize; concierge onboarding (Adam Fishman, Rahul Vohra, Dalton Caldwell, Zoelle Egner)
- **Fishman (Patreon):** route high-potential creators from self-serve to a human, +25% first and second month revenue, then productize the human playbook as defaults and guardrails.
- **Vohra (Superhuman):** one-to-one onboarding of early users beats benchmarks and builds super fans; stop when the top of funnel is too wide, prepare world-class self-service.
- **Caldwell:** Collison Install, finish the last mile of implementation for early customers.
- **Egner:** Airtable's PLG was bootstrapped by high-touch customer success and champions.

### 26. Marginal user and barriers (Adriel Frederick, Naomi Gleit)
- **Marginal user:** study the person on the cusp of the action; find a high-traffic, low-conversion country and walk through its worst-case experience. **Macro vs micro barriers (Gleit):** eligibility, device, access, language versus in-flow friction; with high demand, remove micro friction. Facebook's three core problems: findability, ease of entry, finding friends.

### 27. Agent-era activation (Elena Verna 4.0, Roman Ugarte, Dan Shipper, Scott Wu, Amol Avasare)
- **Wow moment, not aha:** first preview after the first prompt. Activation lives in the core agent team. Avoid the blank-screen problem by steering users to small setup tasks. Roman Ugarte's five things off my plate prompt after connecting Slack and email. Dan Shipper: agent-mediated onboarding, users paste a prompt into their own agent. Capability overhang: build on-ramps to new capabilities.

## C. Retention and engagement

### 28. Hierarchy of Engagement (Sarah Tavel)
- **Levels:** (1) core action, (2) retention: product gets better with use and users have more to lose by leaving, (3) self-perpetuation: usage converts back into the product via network effect, growth loops and re-engagement loops.
- **Measure:** weekly cohorts on both whether users stay active and whether they keep doing the core action; look for the smile graph and a plateau.
- **Anti-patterns:** Evernote skipped level three and had to buy users; pure anonymity fails at level two; real-time push-based products can break their own flywheel with notification overload.

### 29. Early-lifecycle variability and retention sensitivity (Dan Hockenmaier; Jason Cohen; Adam Fishman)
- Find customers having a bad first week or month who should not be, and pull them to average. Chart cancellations by age. Moving a curve early compounds (50% to 55% early lifts final retention 20-30%). Onboarding work can shift curves 10-20 points.

### 30. Cohort tests for retention (Brian Balfour, Josh Miller, Peter Deng, Shaun Clowes)
- Smile curve signals a network effect; cohort-over-cohort improvement beats absolute level; a curve that asymptotes means the product works (Instagram's Bolt did not). Declining cohort curves with no product change signal adjacent, less tech-savvy users arriving (Kaba). Do far more cohort analysis.

### 31. Streak and habit system design (Jackson Shuttleworth, Albert Cheng, Tim Holley, Nir Eyal)
- **Streak:** define on the unit of use; test goal length with opt-out and easier fallback; flexibility (two freezes) with a perfection counterweight; Earn Back instead of paid repair; seven-day focus; explain the mechanic in eight words; reminder 23.5 hours after last practice; core loop before streak.
- **Cheng's three pillars:** core loop, metagame (path, leaderboards), profile. **Protect beginners:** hide ratings for the first five games.
- **Habit loop (Holley):** intent action (favorite) → trigger (sale, only one left) → reward.
- **Nir Eyal:** most products have a nobody-cares problem; use habit design for beneficial behavior.

### 32. Building state and Active Social Network (Julian Shapiro; Mark Pincus)
- **State:** accrue reputation, audience, graph or embedded infrastructure users would lose. Designed in, hard to bolt on.
- **ASN (Zynga):** count round trips with friends; zero to one ASN gave an 80% chance of return next month. Track Day 365 retention, and treat viral companies as sinking speedboats unless retention is high.

### 33. Tactical retention and cancellation flows (Patrick Campbell)
- **Claim:** tactical retention (failed payments, cancel flows, offboarding) is 25-40% of churn past PMF and takes about two months.
- **Cancel flow:** 18-30 seconds, two multiple-choice questions (why leaving, what did you like), then a salvage, pause or maintenance offer. Barbell usage: daily workflow or no-login-needed products retain best; the middle churns most.
- **Related:** pause button (Widjaja), auto-cancel inactive users (Gibson Biddle), product usage benchmarks as retention value (Sahil Mansuri).

### 34. Churn diagnosis (Jason Cohen, Bob Moesta, Vijay Iyengar, Madhavan Ramanujam)
- Too expensive and project ended are never the real reason. Look for differential signals between good and bad customers. Reach out to off-path customers before they cancel. Interview churned users about what changed in their context. Separate lost need from competitor feature gaps. Acquire customers who will not leave.

### 35. Resurrection: when and how (Dan Hockenmaier, Albert Cheng)
- **Hockenmaier:** low leverage early; churned users already decided against you.
- **Cheng:** mature products have a large pool of current plus resurrected users; build a dedicated returning-user experience (social triggers, fresh placement test). Choose by maturity.

## D. Loops, acquisition and channels

### 36. Growth loops and loops-not-funnels (Elena Verna, Shishir Mehrotra, Hari Srinivasan, Casey Winters)
- **Steps:** identify an action that creates a reaction that generates another action; make it self-contained; resource as an engine. Write the loop and name it (Coda Black Loop and Blue Loop). Rank entry points: teammate share, published content, cold. Look at what you pitched to recent candidates to find it.
- **Unify B2B loops (Winters):** merge self-serve and sales loops so sales picks up product-qualified leads.

### 37. Product-led acquisition (Julian Shapiro)
- **Categories:** settle a debt, invite into a conversation, billboarding, user-generated content. Zero marginal cost, compounding, in your control.
- **Referral programs are not PLA** (they attract reward-seekers). Fails: enterprise sales-led businesses.

### 38. Referral and word-of-mouth realities (Sean Ellis, Rahul Vohra, Oji Udezue, Nilan Peiris, Merci Grace, Albert Cheng, Tim Holley)
- Referral is an accelerant when talk already exists. No app sustained a viral factor above 1. Synthetic virality fails on weak products. NPS overlaid on referral shows people told doubles at each band; share NPS comments weekly. Target the connector persona for invites. Track where users already screenshot and polish those moments. Pay referral in cheap in-product credit (Etsy listing credits). Wise's biggest referral gain (3x sharing) came from making savings believable, not changing the incentive.

### 39. Earned channels first (Elena Verna 3.0)
- Prioritize virality, referrals and UGC over rented SEO, SEM and social; staff product and engineering. Companions: B2B report sharing, push and pull into the account, community as amplifier, influencer video as amplification of existing word of mouth.

### 40. Channel selection and exploration (Adam Grenier, Yuriy Timen, Sean Ellis, Jonathan Lowenhar, Melissa Tan, Luc Levesque)
- **Grenier's three ingredients:** strength of the medium versus customer need, the channel's own DNA (trajectory, monetization), your company DNA (risk appetite, staffing, Google/Facebook volume). New channels pay off about 5% of the time.
- **Timen's archetypes:** strong LTV and above about 5% free-to-paid points to paid; inherent network effects to referral and viral; programmatic or data angle to SEO. Guardrails: define impressions, angles and CTR in advance. 80/20 channel concentration is normal; diversify only once volume is meaningful; funding climate sets the mix.
- **Ellis:** demand generation versus demand harvesting; ask customers how they found you. **Lowenhar:** Traction list, brainwriting, impact-effort, 3-4 experiments to beat availability bias. **Levesque:** most companies win by dominating one loop.

### 41. Paid acquisition rules (Gina Gotthilf, Grant Lee, Yuriy Timen, Archie Abrams, Jonathan Becker, Timothy Davis, Gokul Rajaram, Laura Modi)
- Paid decays as you scale (Winters). No paid before LTV (Gotthilf); paid under 50% of acquisition (Lee); 40-50% organic (Rajaram). Two reasons to run paid: efficient returns or fast learning (Timen). Incrementality over attribution (Abrams). Send platforms signals tied to business outcomes (Davis, Austin Hay). Avoid the cost-per-lead trap and pipe CRM revenue back for lead scoring (Becker). Platform sequencing: Google Search, Meta, YouTube, TikTok if data shows users, LinkedIn last unless enterprise. Fix the post-click funnel, not just the ad (Gotthilf). Skeleton before makeup (Meltem Kuran Berkowitz).

### 42. Four-step platform distribution cycle (Brian Balfour)
- Competitive market → one player finds the moat → platform opens with a value exchange → platform closes for control and monetization. You cannot opt out (prisoner's dilemma); go early and plan the exit.

### 43. Community playbooks (Camille Ricketts, Elena Verna, Claire Butler, Jason Lemkin)
- **Two-by-two:** pre-PMF enterprise: customer advisory boards; PMF enterprise: champions and consultant ecosystem; PMF consumer: ambassadors and influencers. Start with 20 vocal users, induct about 20 ambassadors a month, host inside the tool (Slack). Do not hold early community to metrics. Atomic unit of sharing: what your product creates that people want to show off. Customer events ladder (dinner, meetup, theater). VP of Free: own the non-converting long tail.

### 44. Influencer programs (Grant Lee)
- $10-20K a month for six months across 20-40 micro-influencers; onboard like customers, open-source your brand kit; LinkedIn converted 4-5x others for Gamma. Venn diagram free-product seeding (Lulu Cheng Meservey).

### 45. Marketplace and supply-side growth (Sarah Tavel, Benjamin Lauzier, Tim Holley, Dan Hockenmaier)
- Tipping loops: growth loops plus happiness loops. Lyft mentor program ($35 per session) and driver recruiters ($20 per activation). Seed supply from existing channels and in person (Etsy craft fairs). Guarantee first-week earnings.

## E. PLG, sales and expansion

### 46. PLG fit and readiness (Hila Qu, Merci Grace, Carilu Dietrich, Chris Miller)
- **Fit:** low complexity, short time to value, large pool of end users. **Readiness:** free vehicle, fast time to value, self-checkout, data foundation, simple pricing. **Reality:** PLG is data-led growth; a free trial alone is not PLG; dedicated team, one-to-two year roadmap. Modular PLG: place humans where cost is defensible. Ask why you want to be product led.

### 47. Product-led sales and PQA (Elena Verna, Naomi Ionita, Carilu Dietrich, Hila Qu)
- Self-serve caps near $10K; PLS converts usage into a pipeline for 15K-100K+ contracts. Detect PQA, hit across channels, sunset, wait. Sales engages after a threshold (20-40 engaged users). Rank accounts by upgrade likelihood; surface neglected accounts. Individual to team to enterprise escalator.

### 48. Bottoms-up enterprise and champions (Claire Butler, Noah Weiss, Ivan Zhao, Zoelle Egner, Yuhki Yamashita, Roman Ugarte)
- Win ICs who love the tool; make it easy to try and share; turn the enterprise blocker into the strategy (Figma design systems); sort customer domains by active users and call the largest (Slack); B2C2B (half of Notion's B2B customers came from personal users); champions not buyers (Airtable). Needs technical or craft audiences with solo value and an existing community.

### 49. PLG ceiling and hybrid (Jeanne DeWitt Grosser, Pete Kazanjy, Geoffrey Moore, Laura Schaffer, Shaun Clowes)
- Self-serve has a deal-size ceiling; add sales before you hit it. Never mistake lead gen for the business. PLG cannot cross the chasm alone. PLG and SLG are two sides of one coin; do not chop up the enterprise product for self-serve.

### 50. Land and expand fence (Madhavan Ramanujam; Hila Qu)
- Ninety percent of claimed land-and-expand companies only land. Gate by features and usage; leave something for expansion. Three expansion buckets: tier upgrade, seats, consumption add-ons.
