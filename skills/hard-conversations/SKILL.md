# Hard Conversations

![hard-conversations: lead guest team](assets/card.png)

Turn a conversation you are dreading into a short, specific script you can say out loud, plus a plan for how they will react. Built from 183 insights from 66 Lenny's Podcast guests. **Lead team:** Kim Scott, Carole Robin, Alisa Cohn.

## When to use
- You owe someone feedback and keep putting it off (a report, a peer, a designer, an engineer, your boss).
- A report keeps missing deadlines, interrupting people, or not meeting the bar, and softer hints have not worked.
- You are denying a promotion, delivering a bad review, or heading toward a firing or a layoff.
- A peer or cross-functional partner is damaging your work (excluding you, undercutting you) and you are getting resentful.
- You just got hard feedback and need to decide how to respond, or you want real feedback and everyone says everything is fine.
- A conversation went sideways (tears, anger, defensiveness) and you need a repair.
- You need to say no to a request without damaging the relationship.

If the user attached anything (a draft message, review text, Slack thread, 1:1 notes, performance doc), read it first and work from it. Do not ask for what the file already answers.

## Step 1 — Diagnose (ask before answering)
Ask only what you cannot infer. Max 4 questions:
1. **Who is it with, and how much trust and history is there?** Report / peer / boss / exec / cross-functional; how often do you praise them specifically? *Why:* low trust means build the reservoir and the opt-in first (Alisa Cohn, Julie Zhuo); high trust allows blunter delivery (Kim Scott, Ebi Atawodi).
2. **What kind of conversation is it?** A one-off moment, a recurring pattern, a gap against stated expectations, a promotion no, a pre-exit warning, friction that is mostly about how it feels, or you are the one receiving? *Why:* this picks the play in Step 2.
3. **What is your evidence, and have you stated the expectation clearly?** What would a video camera have recorded? Have you ever said the bar out loud? *Why:* unclear expectations turn this into a clarity conversation, not a performance one (Molly Graham, Ben Horowitz, Alisa Cohn).
4. **What outcome do you want, and what makes this hard for you?** (Fear of tears, venting, protecting your reputation, fear they will quit.) *Why:* it exposes which failure mode you are in (ruinous empathy vs aggression) and whether you should wait before speaking.

## Step 2 — Pick the play
| If… (situation) | Use | From | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small, fresh moment (meeting, doc, demo) | **CORE + HHIIPPP** (Context, Observation, Result, Next step; do it now, in person or on the phone) | Kim Scott | Fast, behavior-focused, works for praise and criticism |
| Recurring behavior, relationship matters, you are getting irritated | **Behavior-feeling-reason + stay on your side of the net** | Carole Robin | Keeps you to what you actually know; builds the relationship |
| You need a structured opener for a report or peer, minutes of prep | **SBI, 4 Is, or What / So What / Now What** | Paige Costello, Matt Abrahams | Light structures that end in problem solving |
| Performance gap against expectations | **Performance feedback script**, after the expectation-clarity check | Alisa Cohn, Molly Graham | Evidence, link to their goals, agree on the fix |
| Pattern persists after clear feedback; exit is possible | **Pre-firing 30-day conversation**, then a short firing conversation | Alisa Cohn, Matt Mochary | No surprises; HR and legal involved |
| You are denying a promotion | **Promotion-denied script** (decision first, two reasons, hope) | Alisa Cohn | Short, clear, leaves a path |
| The person is defensive, angry, or crying | **Pause script, curious-not-furious, move up on care** | Alisa Cohn, Kim Scott | Hold the message, change the temperature |
| It went badly or you lost your temper | **Repair** (own it, name impact, say what changes) | Becky Kennedy, Matt Abrahams, Carole Robin | Trust is built by repair, not perfection |
| You are the one asking or receiving feedback | **Go-to question, count to six, reward the candor** | Kim Scott, Jules Walter, Nikhyl Singhal | Most feedback you want never arrives unless you ask well |
| You need to decline a request | **Say no by talking about trade-offs** | Wes Kao | Gets the no without the word |

## Step 3 — Run it

### 0. Prep (always, ten minutes)
1. **Motive check.** Are you helping them improve or venting? If venting, wait (Alisa Cohn, Julie Zhuo). Vent first to a friend or partner, then cut about 90% of what you wanted to say; keep only what drives behavior change (Wes Kao).
2. **Worry audit.** Split your hesitation: which part is about their feelings, which part is about how you will look (Kim Scott). The second is self-protection, not kindness.
3. **Evidence.** Write 2-3 observable facts with dates (*Tuesday, 3:00 meeting, interrupted me three times*). If you cannot, you are holding a judgment, not feedback (Alisa Cohn, Kenneth Berger). Swap labels (rude, self-involved, not strategic) for countable behaviors.
4. **Expectation check.** Did they know the bar? If you were unclear, run a clarity conversation first (Molly Graham, Ben Horowitz: when you want to yell, you probably have not explained it).
5. **Outcome.** One sentence: *by the end, we agree that ___*. Add an ask, not just a complaint (Kenneth Berger).
6. **Stretch only 15%.** Say something slightly outside your comfort zone, no further (Carole Robin).
7. **Timing and channel.** Do it soon, in person or by phone, never Slack or email. Delay only if either of you is too upset (Kim Scott). Praise in public, criticize in private by default (see debates).

### A. Impromptu feedback: CORE (Kim Scott)
1. **Context:** where and when. **Observation:** what you saw or heard. **Result:** the impact. **Next step:** what to do. For praise, the next step is *do more of that*.
2. Start neutral with a question (*were you aware of it?*), offer help, and escalate directness only if they brush it off.
3. Frame it as your read, not the truth; ask how they see it (Kim Scott).
4. Done when they can say back the behavior and the next step.

### B. Interpersonal feedback: behavior-feeling-reason (Carole Robin)
1. Three realities: your intent, your behavior, their impact. You only know your own side and the shared behavior. Never narrate their motives.
2. Script: *when you [specific behavior], I feel [one feeling word], and I'm telling you because [outcome I hope for]*. Then ask what happened on their side and problem-solve.
3. **Net test:** if your sentence needs *like* or *that* after *I feel*, you crossed the net. Replace it with a single feeling word (sad, irritated, anxious).
4. **Pinch before crunch:** if irritation is growing, raise it now while small. If you have wanted to say it for three or four days, you are out of integrity (Kenneth Berger).
5. Resolve the interpersonal friction before you give task feedback, or the task feedback becomes a proxy (Carole Robin).

### C. Performance gap (Alisa Cohn, Molly Graham, Becky Kennedy)
1. Open neutral: *I wanted to have a conversation about something I have been observing.*
2. State 2-3 facts, then why it matters to **their** goals. Anneka Gupta also starts by asking where they want to go in their career.
3. Hold identity and behavior apart: this is a good person doing a late-PRD pattern (Becky Kennedy). Say it is a pattern, name the business need, then ask what is going on.
4. Check clarity (Molly Graham): *what do you see as your role and what success looks like?* If their answer differs from yours, re-clarify before judging.
5. Invite their view, then agree on what changes, by when, and how you will both know.
6. Offer help (examples, a class, a pairing); do not take over the task.

### D. The harder variants
- **Promotion denied (Alisa Cohn):** say it will be hard to hear and state the decision in the first sentence; give two reasons; say it matters that they succeed; commit to concrete help; offer to revisit next week. If they argue tenure or fairness, mirror, restate the criteria, and say the decision stands.
- **Pre-firing (Alisa Cohn):** *we have to have a difficult conversation.* Recap that you raised it multiple times over months, cite current evidence, give a deadline (about 30 days) and the consequence, and affirm what they bring. Involve HR and legal. Test: if the honest answer to *have I been crystal clear* is *sort of*, you have not.
- **Firing and layoffs:** short and simple. Recap prior conversations, state the decision, hand logistics to HR. The person hears it from their manager one-on-one, never by email or in a group (Matt Mochary). Offer to be their agent with your network.
- **Environment-fit framing (Kevin Yien):** adapt to this environment or find one that fits better. It is empowering, not a verdict.
- **Specific-role gap (Ben Horowitz):** name what they do well, then the specific requirement of the role they are missing, the concrete consequence, and your offer to help.

### E. When it gets hot
1. **Warn first:** one sentence of notice that it will be a difficult conversation lowers the threat response (Matt Mochary).
2. **Defensive:** pause. *Let's pause for a second; the energy has changed. I'm saying this to help you.* Offer to continue now or later. Hold the point (Alisa Cohn).
3. **Angry:** be curious, not furious. Ask why they are so angry while keeping the issue on the table (Kim Scott). Anger distances; name the underlying hurt or fear if it is yours (Carole Robin).
4. **Sad or crying:** move up on care without retracting. *I may not have said that in the best way; how could I have said it differently?* Do not say *no big deal* (Kim Scott). Tears are not a reason to withhold; Alisa Cohn's client was thanked the next day.
5. **They say the facts are wrong:** list every specific problem and show the pattern (Kim Scott). Ask what they heard you say; it often differs from what you said (Carole Robin).
6. **Close:** restate agreement, who does what, and when you will check in.

### F. Asking and receiving feedback
1. **Ask presumptively**, in your own words, not *do you have any feedback*. Kim Scott's go-to: *what could I do or stop doing that would make it easier to work with me?* Alternatives: *what could I have done this week to better support you?* or a specific behavior (Jules Walter: *to what extent did I show executive presence?*). Offer your own critique first so they can agree.
2. **Count to six** in silence. If still nothing, ask them to notice something before next time.
3. **Listen to understand,** repeat it back more sharply than they said it, ask *is that fair?* (Nikhyl Singhal).
4. **Respond:** agree with the 5-10% you can, say you will think about the rest, then return with a respectful explanation. Or choose: act as stated, look for themes, or keep your style and explain the trade-offs (Ami Vora).
5. **Reward the candor:** fix it, make it visible, name who told you, then ask if you over- or under-corrected (Kim Scott, Nikhyl Singhal).
6. **Mine the discard pile:** the feedback you wrote off as odd is often the shadow of your strength (Nikhyl Singhal).

### G. Declining a request (Wes Kao)
*Yes, I can do that. It means [current work] moves to [date]. Does that work, or should I stay on the original?* A prioritization conversation, not a refusal.

## Where the experts disagree
- **Criticize in private (Kim Scott, Elizabeth Stone) vs in public (Jeetu Patel, Keith Rabois).** *Use private when trust is not yet established, the issue is personal, or the person is sensitive. Use public only on established-trust teams where the goal is the best idea winning and visible problem-solving.* Default: private; keep praise public.
- **Be soft and warm (Alisa Cohn's even-keeled opener, Boz: be kind, not nice) vs just say it (Claire Vo: not meeting expectations, Kim Scott's escalation).** *Soften tone, never content.* If hints have failed, go to Claire Vo's plain version: state the gap, the consequence, that you believe in them, and that it is their responsibility.
- **Feelings first (Carole Robin, Rachel Lockett) vs facts only (Alisa Cohn, Kenneth Berger).** *Use a feeling word when the relationship is the issue and you are close; stay on facts and impact for performance conversations with reports.* Both reject judgments.
- **Do it now (Kim Scott) vs wait until calm (Alisa Cohn, Julie Zhuo).** *Wait only for your motive or heat, not your nerves.* Name your nervousness out loud (Julie Zhuo).
- **Just say thank you (Jules Walter) vs thanks is not enough (Kim Scott).** *Say thanks immediately; reward it with a visible fix.*
- **Public, recorded, relentless critique (Dalio-style) vs Kim Scott:** the latter calls it obnoxious aggression. Candor is judged at the listener's ear.

```markdown
**Outcome I want:** [one sentence]
**Motive check:** [helping / venting], my worry about myself: [one line]

## Evidence (camera-level facts)
1. [date, situation, behavior]  2. [...]  3. [...]

- Opener (notice + intent): [...]
- Observation / behavior: [...]
- Impact (my feeling or the result): [...]
- Tie to their goal: [...]
- Invite their view: [...]
- Ask / next step: [...]

## If they react
| Reaction | My line | Hold the point? |
|---|---|---|
| Defensive | [pause script] | yes |
| Angry | [curious question] | yes |
| Sad / crying | [care without retracting] | yes |
| Disagrees with facts | [list the pattern, ask what they heard] | yes |

## Logistics
Channel: [in person/phone] | When: [date] | Who else needs to know (HR, manager): [...]
Rehearsal: [mirror, then a trio with a partner and observer for net jumping]

## Next 3 actions
1. [Rehearse out loud and cut 90%]  2. [Hold the conversation by DATE]  3. [Follow-up check-in date and what I will look for]
```

## Red flags
- **Ruinous empathy** is where about 90% of our mistakes live (Kim Scott); softening a likable underperformer cost one team its best people.
- **Hinting.** Passive-aggressive feedback is the worst kind (Anneka Gupta); *I've gotten some feedback that you could improve* is unkind (Claire Vo).
- **Net jumping:** labels, *you don't care*, *I feel like* statements (Carole Robin, Rachel Lockett).
- **Slack, email, or a small video square** for hard feedback (Kim Scott).
- **Asking for accountability** as a speech; it triggers safety systems and facade compliance (Evan LaPointe). Ask questions instead (Varun Parmar).
- **Surprise firings.** Founders who knew in month one and did nothing (Uri Levine); no 30-day conversation (Alisa Cohn).
- **Venting dressed as feedback** (Alisa Cohn, Wes Kao).
- **Radical Candor as a license to be a jerk,** or public relentless critique (Kim Scott).
- **Retracting when they look sad** (Kim Scott), or arguing back when you disagree with feedback.
- **Never asking.** Ask for the thing you hear in calibration rooms but never to your face (Jules Walter).
