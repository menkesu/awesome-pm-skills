# Frameworks: Hard Conversations

Grouped by theme. Originators credited; other endorsers noted.

## 1. Mindset and prep

**Care personally, challenge directly: Radical Candor** (Kim Scott, Kim Scott episode) — A 2x2: care personally (vertical) by challenge directly (horizontal). Missing care gives obnoxious aggression; missing challenge gives ruinous empathy; missing both gives manipulative insincerity. Use it as a compass for one specific conversation, not as a label for people. Fails when used as a license to be a jerk. Endorsed by Maya Prohovnik, Boz, Ebi Atawodi. Steps: plot the conversation; name its failure mode; move toward care plus the hard thing. Benchmark: most mistakes (Kim Scott: roughly 90%) are ruinous empathy.

**Mindset check before the conversation** (Alisa Cohn) — Name what is uncomfortable and the meaning you attach (they will be upset, more work for me); list other possible outcomes (growth, relief, trust); check whether you are helping or venting; if venting, wait. Applies to conflict-averse managers. Julie Zhuo's version: check intention (about 80% of the battle) and wait until calm.

**Worry audit** (Kim Scott) — Separate worry about their feelings (ruinous empathy) from worry about your own reputation (manipulative insincerity). Withholding usually mixes both.

**Strategy, not self-expression** (Wes Kao) — Goal is behavior change; cut about 90% of what you want to say; keep what motivates change and shows the benefit. Vent beforehand to a friend, partner or therapist.

**Integrity check** (Kenneth Berger) — If you have kept thinking for three or four days that you need to say something to someone, say it. Notice unowned feelings (annoyed, frustrated, nervous).

**Radical Candor plus an ask** (Kenneth Berger) — Convert the complaint into a communicated desire for a specific outcome likely to get a yes. People pleasers hint and hope; control freaks order; both are ruts.

**Pinch before it becomes a crunch** (Carole Robin) — If irritation is increasing, raise it now using behavior-feeling-reason. Test the thought it is not worth it by substituting *we are not worth it*. If irritation fades, no need to raise it.

**The 15% rule** (Carole Robin) — Stretch about 15% outside your comfort zone when disclosing or giving feedback; the zone resets and you go another 15%.

**Where you come from matters more than what you say** (Joe Hudson) — Check whether you are speaking from fear or from care. Find who the boss listens to and observe how they raise objections.

**Ambivalence as a signal** (Rachel Lockett) — Avoidance means something important is at stake. Ask what is important here and what you have to learn. Not for grinding through depleting work.

**Take 100% responsibility** (Rachel Lockett) — Ask what you contribute to the dynamic; avoid victim, blame, or hero modes.

**Withholding is selfish / unkind** (Matt MacInnis, Kim Scott) — Reframe withholding as optimizing for your comfort at their expense; recall your own Bob story (a softened hire who failed and later said he never saw the firing coming).

## 2. Delivering feedback

**CORE: Context, Observation, Result, Next step** (Kim Scott) — Use for praise and criticism. Example shape: context (in the meeting), observation (um every third word), result (it made you sound stupid), next step (a speech coach). Praise next step is do more of that.

**HHIIPPP** (Kim Scott) — Humble, Helpful, Immediate, In person (synchronous), Praise in public, Criticize in private, Not about Personality. For short impromptu feedback. Phone beats video for gauging how it lands; Slack is a train wreck.

**Start neutral, escalate directness** (Kim Scott) — Open with a question (were you aware of it?), offer help, escalate only if they deflect. Her Google boss did this until her brush-offs stopped.

**Three realities and the net** (Carole Robin) — Intent, behavior, impact. You know your intent and the shared behavior; you do not know their intent. Stay on your side of the net (feelings, reactions) and never cross into blame or attribution. Rachel Lockett credits this to Stanford's Touchy Feely course. Fails: shifts probabilities, no guarantees; may need repair.

**Behavior-feeling-reason formula** (Carole Robin) — When you do X, I feel Y, and I'm telling you because Z. Then problem-solve with them. Works for complimentary feedback too: be specific rather than saying nice job.

**I feel like / I feel that test** (Carole Robin, Rachel Lockett) — Any sentence that needs like or that after I feel contains an attribution or fact. Use a single emotion word.

**Feedback is data: constructive or complimentary** (Carole Robin) — Drop positive and negative. Constructive leads to problem solving; complimentary is specific appreciation.

**Interpersonal before task** (Carole Robin) — Unresolved interpersonal friction contaminates task feedback. Wait until after the meeting, ask permission, describe behavior, impact, and how it undermined their stated goal.

**SBI: Situation, Behavior, Impact** (Paige Costello) — Impact is your subjective experience (made me feel unheard), which makes it valid and opens next steps. Also the shape of Fishman's competency feedback.

**The 4 Is: Information, Impact, Invitation, Implications** (Matt Abrahams) — Information sets the field, impact on you, invitation to solve together, implications (benefits or consequences). Vary tone and directness. Related: What / So What / Now What for fast post-meeting feedback.

**Nonviolent Communication: observation, feeling, need, request** (Rachel Lockett; similar to DBT DEAR MAN per Kenneth Berger) — Observation you could photograph, a non-blaming feeling, a universal need (clarity, collaboration), a small easy request as an olive branch. Goal is mutual understanding, not winning. Fails if you arrive rigid and blaming.

**Factual ask: camera, thoughts, feelings, want** (Kenneth Berger) — State what a video camera would record, what you think, what you feel, what you want.

**Observable facts, not judgments** (Alisa Cohn) — Ask what is my evidence; frame as what I have observed; state the expectation and the gap; show examples of others doing it well; offer help. Harder for feedback about feelings.

**Frame feedback as perception** (Anneka Gupta) — This is how I and others perceive you; it may not be intended. Give three concrete things to change and brainstorm together. Start by asking where they want to go in their career.

**Direct feedback with intention** (Melissa Tan) — Restate intention, say you believe in them, offer help, describe observation, impact and alternative; do it early (she pulled someone aside two or three weeks in).

**Give problems, not solutions** (Julie Zhuo) — Articulate the problem with examples; let the owner propose solutions.

**Candor, not truth** (Kim Scott) — Go in humble: it is your read; ask for theirs.

**Three tactics for delivering hard feedback** (Julie Zhuo) — Opt-in upfront (about 60% of the battle), intention check (about 80%), and name your nervousness out loud.

**Be kind, not nice** (Boz) — Nice is patronizing half-truths; kind is direct and helpful with belief it can be fixed.

**Say the thing you cannot say** (Claire Hughes Johnson) — Ask a question; own the observation as yours; avoid judgment; others were probably holding the same thought.

**Accountability as questions** (Varun Parmar) — State what you observe, admit you may be missing context, ask them to help you understand. Use on leadership teams with trust. Evan LaPointe warns that announcing accountability backfires.

**Hold people to potential** (Tobi Lutke; similar Brian Chesky) — Name the fork where they took the convenient path and what you expect next time. Needs high trust.

**Process gap naming** (Nancy Duarte) — Most conflict is about process, not goals. Name it and ask whose process to use.

## 3. Identity, belief and tone

**Good inside: separate behavior from identity** (Becky Kennedy) — Hold identity (a good person) apart from behavior (late). Script: same team, you are a good person, it has been consistent, here is why it matters, what is making it hard, let us solve it. Boundaries and consequences still apply.

**I believe you / I believe in you** (Becky Kennedy) — Validate the experience and express confidence in capability together. Diagnose which half you default to. Fails: only validating or only minimizing.

**Clear is kind** (Claire Vo; Kim Scott) — State not meeting expectations, the consequence, belief in them, and their responsibility to change. Use when softer feedback has failed.

**Be loved, not liked** (Ebi Atawodi) — Repeatedly show you care about the person behind the role; then raw feedback lands as caring.

**Seeing potential** (Brian Chesky) — Not good enough signals belief in their potential. Fails with fixed-mindset teams.

**Environment-fit framing** (Kevin Yien) — You are not bad; the machine you are in may not suit you; adapt or find a better one.

**Name their superpower** (Alex Komoroske) — Early in the relationship, state a hypothesis of their superpower so nudges feel like help being more themselves.

**Specific-role feedback** (Ben Horowitz) — Name the gap against the role (great director of engineering, not yet an effective CTO), the consequence, and your offer; state you will hire for the role if it does not close.

**Behavior can change; can't is a choice** (Carole Robin) — Add yet to I can't; accept I do not want to.

## 4. Performance, exits and hard calls

**Performance feedback script** (Alisa Cohn) — Open neutral, state observed facts, link to their goals, invite their view, close with how they will fix it. Needs prior trust (build with specific praise).

**Build the reservoir of goodwill** (Alisa Cohn) — Prepare praise as carefully as criticism; say keep doing that; do it routinely.

**Expectation clarity check** (Molly Graham; Alisa Cohn; Ben Horowitz) — Ask what they think their role and success are; re-clarify (you are in charge of the trunk); only then treat it as a performance gap. If you have said sort of clear, you have not.

**Promotion-denied script** (Alisa Cohn) — Decision first, two reasons, commitment to their success, concrete help, revisit next week; mirror then hold the decision.

**Pre-firing 30-day conversation** (Alisa Cohn) — Difficult conversation, recap, evidence, deadline and consequence, affirm. HR and legal. Matt Mochary and Uri Levine reinforce no surprises; Uri adds asking top performers how sore they would feel if the person left.

**Keeper test** (Elizabeth Stone, from Netflix) — If this person said they were leaving, would I fight to keep them or feel relief? Use as an entry point for feedback in both directions (how am I doing on your keeper test). Can cause anxiety without regular feedback.

**Expectations, specific feedback, help close the gap** (Elizabeth Stone) — State expectations, give direct feedback on what reaching the bar takes, jump in and help, deliver in private.

**No-emotions test** (Lenny, endorsed by Molly Graham) — If nobody got upset, what would I do? Do that, then deliver it kindly.

**Humane layoff delivery** (Matt Mochary) — Manager, one-on-one, 15 minutes; follow-up hour to be their agent. Litmus: you stay friends with everyone you have let go.

**Sprint output questions** (Nikita Miller) — Ask what they delivered, what reached production, how long it took, the cycle time, so people see low output themselves.

## 5. When it gets hot, and repair

**Difficult conversation script** (Matt Mochary) — Warn them, deliver, name likely emotions and invite them, listen, then explicitly accept or decline their feedback. Surprise triggers the amygdala.

**I perceive you to be in X** (Matt Mochary) — An I-statement of perception, not an accusation or question.

**Defensiveness pause script** (Alisa Cohn) — Pause, reaffirm the help intent, name the change in energy in words they accept, offer now or later, hold the point.

**Curious, not furious; move up on care** (Kim Scott) — If angry, get curious. If sad, ask how you could have said it better; do not retract.

**What did you hear me say?** (Carole Robin) — Nine times out of ten it differs from what you said; do not argue with the interpretation; restate.

**Repair** (Becky Kennedy) — Go back, own your part, name impact, say what you will do differently. Fails when used to explain away.

**AAA apology** (Matt Abrahams) — Acknowledge, Appreciate the impact, Amends. Apologize for the transgression, not for how they felt.

**Anger is a distancing emotion** (Carole Robin) — Name the connecting emotion (hurt, fear, sadness) underneath.

**Fascinating, tell me more** (Boz) — Respond with genuine curiosity to deep disagreement; be willing to change your mind.

**Identity threat** (Boz) — Learn the bodily signal; repair afterward.

**Show an alternative way to say it** (Christian Idiodi) — With a leader's harsh style, ask permission, restate their message better, let the team say which worked.

**Face-to-face repair** (Ethan Evans) — Angry emails are easy; sit near them and let a human conversation happen.

## 6. Asking for and receiving feedback

**Go-to question** (Kim Scott) — Presumptive, in your own words (what could I do or stop doing that would make it easier to work with me). Do not ask do you have any feedback. Calendar it; practice in a mirror. Adjust if your authenticity shuts people down. Time-bound and specific variants for people who dislike open questions.

**Count to six** (Kim Scott) — Stay silent; if nothing, ask them to notice something for next time.

**Order of operations** (Kim Scott) — Solicit first, then praise and criticize.

**Reward the candor** (Kim Scott; Nikhyl Singhal; Jules Walter) — Fix it, be loud about who told you, thank them, ask if you over- or under-corrected. Debate: Jules Walter stresses enthusiastic gratitude; Kim Scott says thanks alone is not enough.

**Disagreeing with feedback** (Kim Scott) — Voice the 5-10% you agree with, promise to think, return with a respectful explanation.

**Repeat it back better** (Nikhyl Singhal; steelmanning per Lane Shackleton) — Restate more clearly than they said it; ask if it is a fair assessment.

**Receiving hard feedback** (Anneka Gupta) — Feel it, do not react, get curious, decide whether to act (all feedback is valid; not all needs action).

**Choose how to respond** (Ami Vora) — Act as stated, look for themes, or keep your style and give context. Expand your toolset; do not shrink yourself.

**Take a punch / counter-program the narrative** (Hilary Gridley) — One small action showing the opposite of what you fear they think.

**Mine the discard pile** (Nikhyl Singhal) — Review feedback you dismissed as anomalies; they are artifacts of your strength.

**Shadows of superpowers** (Nikhyl Singhal) — Pairs: collaborators hold few opinions; listeners struggle to decide; storytellers miss details; structured thinkers struggle with blue sky.

**Ground-truth feedback** (Nikhyl Singhal) — Informal peer conversations differ dramatically from formal feedback; triangulate and create safety.

**Make it safe to give feedback** (Jules Walter) — Ask about one behavior, self-critique first, thank them visibly; seek subjective EQ feedback.

**Require constructive feedback in 1:1s and T3 B3** (Keith Yandell) — Reserve 1:1 time for constructive input; Uber reviews listed three positives and three constructive points about your manager.

**Filter criticism by track record** (Kunal Shah) — React to critics who have done better than you and understand the nuance. Not for customer feedback.

**Open feedback-to-the-leader session** (Varun Parmar) — Run a one-to-two-hour open session at offsites.

**Listening levels** (Heidi Helfand) — Internal, focused on the other person, global awareness of body language and mood.

**Say no with trade-offs** (Wes Kao) — Yes, and it means X moves to later; ask which they prefer. For ongoing relationships, not strangers.

**Personal OS** (Donna Lichaw) — Tell your team how you process so silence is not misread.

**Shred this** (Maggie Crowley) — Hand your doc to engineering and ask them to rip it apart.

**Public criticism, when it works** (Jeetu Patel, Keith Rabois) — Contrarian to praise public, criticize private: only with established trust, aimed at best-idea-wins; Rabois mixes in private feedback.
