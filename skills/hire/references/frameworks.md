# Hire: frameworks

Every framework below comes from Lenny's Podcast guests. Format: **Name** (originator, episode) — purpose; when it applies or fails; steps; benchmark.

## 1. Define the role before you source

**Scoping before sourcing** (Adam Ward, Adam Ward) — Objectively define great before opening a role, because scoping drives sourcing, the pitch, the assessment and the close. Applies to any important role. Steps: list required skills and experiences and stack-rank them; write why the role matters and what success looks like; use that to craft sourcing, pitch, assessment criteria and close. Related: Ward also warns against defining great by logos or mirroring other companies' processes (logos are occasional shorthand only when a specific team at a specific time was truly great). Endorsed by Gokul Rajaram, who says to spend the first four or five hours designing the hiring process, messaging and target profiles before reaching out.

**Major, minor, spike and outcome-first definition** (Lauren Ipsen, Lauren Ipsen) — For senior product searches. Decide where the leader majors, minors and spikes (design, senior-PM operator, long-term vision). Steps: ask what outcome the hire solves for and their mandate; describe what crushing it looks like; define 90-day, 1-year and 2-year expectations and whether they could grow to CPO; map product direction at 12 months, 18 months and through IPO. Fails when the board mandates a head of product and no one can name the problem: delay the search.

**12-months-later outcome statement** (Jonathan Lowenhar, Jonathan Lowenhar) — Instead of a job description, write what success looks like a year after hire (imagine clinking champagne), document what changed in the business, and interview for people who have already done that. For juniors, look for adjacent evidence (the example given: selling Girl Scout cookies).

**Define the job before the job description** (Ken Norton, Ken Norton) — Hiring managers often don't mean the same thing by 'product manager'. Talk through what the role really is, check shared understanding across stakeholders, and make sure the candidate hears the real scope (not a feature factory).

**CEO questionnaire before hiring a CPO** (Manik Gupta, Manik Gupta) — Ask the CEO what they want to spend time on; if they want to keep owning roadmap and execution, don't hire a CPO. Clarify whether you need process, strategy or talent attraction, how engineering, data science, design, GM and operations fit, and the CEO/CPO/CTO swim lanes. Companion: Ami Vora's advice to candidates is to spend a day with the CEO before accepting.

**Three product leader archetypes** (Lauren Ipsen) — Platform/infrastructure, core or consumer product (including design-led), and growth or monetization specialists. Pick the bucket matching your most imminent need and hire for it first. Anti-pattern: the unicorn head of product asked to do ads, product marketing and core UX, who will need lieutenants anyway.

**Five PM archetypes** (Peter Deng, Peter Deng) — Consumer PM (designer plus product), growth PM (data scientist plus product), GM/business PM, platform PM, research/AI PM. Identify each team member's primary and secondary archetype, map gaps, match archetype to market (a consumer PM in a boring regulated industry will be frustrated), pair opposing archetypes (consumer vs growth). A lens, not a test.

**Architect, optimizer, scaler** (Jonathan Lowenhar) — Architects build the first playbook, optimizers improve it with 10-15 reps, scalers find 10x leverage. Determine your function's stage and hire the matching archetype; hire an architect only if they have been one. An optimizer in an architect role fails.

**PSHE: Problem, Solution, How, Execution** (Shishir Mehrotra, Shishir Mehrotra) — Locate people by what they are handed versus what they originate. Juniors are handed problem, solution and how; mid-levels figure out the how; seniors bring solutions to a given problem; the most senior define the problems. Applies to product, engineering, design, sales. Mehrotra values P over S over H over E but notes industries may invert that. Related: scope is an input given by a manager, not an output; the level-guide reverse-identification test (strip level labels and ask managers to identify the levels; drop whatever fails to discriminate). Warning: the H-level halo, where a great meeting runner is mistaken for a problem solver.

**Skills, attributes, values** (Oji Udezue, Oji Udezue, from Bridgewater) — Evaluate three dimensions, not skills alone. Write the JD and interview to extract all three. Bridgewater's execution (heavy attrition, constant rating) was not empathetic, so borrow the framing only.

**Job description as an experience** (Bob Moesta, Bob Moesta 2.0) — For every requirement ask what the person will do with it; describe context, what the role is about, what progress means and how you'll reward it; market the role to people not already looking. 'Five years of experience' is lazy: name the capability. Moesta's companion view: fit the job to the person by asking candidates for energy drivers and drains and reshaping the role (works at startups and small teams; fails under legal constraints on JDs).

**Hiring is never the goal** (Jonathan Lowenhar) — Start from a quantified milestone (fundraise, exit, profitability), define the work and resource gap, decide hire versus rent, and define 6-12 month success. Related: Elena Verna wants an ROI for a hire before opening a requisition, proven by doing the channel yourself first.

## 2. Pick the right person for the stage

**Best for this role at this time** (Lauren Ipsen) — Ask who is best for this specific role at this specific time, not who is the best talent in the market. Define the stage and the problem now; evaluate against that, not brand prestige. Early-stage companies need someone closer to the work, who can act as a hands-on PM some days and build a team on others, not a CPO far from the work.

**Hire patiently but not too slowly** (Michael Truell, Michael Truell; Brendan Foody) — Cursor and Mercor both say they hired too slowly in hindsight. Foody: patient for the first 10, then step on the gas when demand far exceeds capacity. Counter: Grant Lee says never set headcount targets and spend heavy time on the first 10 to set the DNA; Ray Cao learned that hiring 100 in six months compromised the bar.

**Undiscovered talent** (Keith Rabois, Keith Rabois) — Startups can't win bidding for proven talent. Ask what this person would be missed for in a large company's homogeneous recruiting black box, often because younger people have few data points. Pair with 'pitch the blocker to their skill': convince proven talent that their specific ability overlaps your critical blocker, so they bet on themselves.

**Barrel** (Keith Rabois) — A barrel takes an idea and makes it happen, and proactively returns early with diagnosis and help requests. Test: give an outcome, expand scope progressively, check that they fire-and-forget your problems. The smoothie story (an intern who delivered smoothies at 9pm after everyone else failed) is an informal ownership test.

**Hiring leverage: one plus one equals three** (Keith Rabois) — Managers show leverage when each hire produces disproportionate returns; assess people you know with context, recruit underused talent from within, and build a separate technique for strangers.

**Promote from within** (Keith Rabois; Gokul Rajaram; Brian Halligan; Dmitry Zlokazov) — Rabois: hire senior outsiders for value preservation, not value creation; Ramp uses chief-of-staff roles to groom leaders. Rajaram: the first PM is best chosen from existing analysts, engineers or designers because outsiders can be rejected like an organ transplant (fails at later stages needing functional PM skill). Halligan: the 2004 Red Sox mix of homegrown talent plus a few proven stars. Zlokazov: internal transfers are a guaranteed culture match.

**Hire the lieutenants** (Gokul Rajaram, Gokul Rajaram) — To hire a function leader, find 3-4 best-in-class companies for a similar customer (not competitors), map org charts, target the lieutenant or lieutenant's lieutenant. Counterpoint from Lauren Ipsen: dig a layer deeper because a team's brand does not mean every person was top 1%.

**Hire from products you love** (Dmitry Zlokazov) — Weekly sourcing sprints targeting companies whose products the team admires; someone who built a great product is likely a high performer. Sri Batchu adds data-driven sourcing (use public data such as SimilarWeb email share of traffic to find strong CRM teams) and a target set one to two stages ahead of you.

**Big-company hire risk** (Brian Halligan; Meltem Kuran Berkowitz; Dmitry Zlokazov; Paul Adams; Emily Kramer) — Executives from large companies expect infrastructure a 50-500 person startup lacks. Ask when the person joined their big employer and at what size; prefer those who were early and did the work. Counterweight (Ben Horowitz): 'never hire anyone with experience' is bad advice where codified trial-and-error knowledge exists (e.g., building a worldwide sales org).

**Hire spiky, team well-rounded** (Brian Halligan; Mihika Kapoor; Peter Sellis) — Panels that average scores keep picking the candidate with the fewest weaknesses; HubSpot's hit rate improved when it hired spikier people and cut the panel from eight to four. Sellis: a product org that cannot absorb spiky talent has failed. Ben Horowitz: invest in strength, not lack of weakness.

**Product talent portfolio** (Eeke de Milliano, Eeke de Milliano) — Every six months chart the team's strengths and weaknesses, hire for the gaps, balance homegrown with outsiders, and push each pillar lead to hire people who don't look like them. Related: Ian Silber's well-rounded design team, Julie Zhuo's skills-needed composition, Nick Turley's executive-style recruiting by team gap.

**Three attributes** (Adam Ward) — Excellent humans and excellent at their craft, with a chip on their shoulder, who put team over self. Mosseri's version: grit, quick learner, self-aware. Peter Deng's: growth mindset, tested in his final interview for everyone.

**AI-era hiring traits** (Nick Turley; Logan Kilpatrick; Kevin Weil; Jenny Wen; Albert Cheng; Dianne Penn; Elena Verna; Caitlin Kalinowski) — Curiosity over prior AI experience (filtering for AI experience selects lucky people); high agency and urgency; comfort with ambiguity and influence; first-principles thinking over pattern matching; hiring AI-native new grads and failed founders; Jenny Wen's three designer archetypes (block-shaped generalist, deep specialist, craft new grad). Keep junior pipelines: Mosseri, Elizabeth Stone, Jeetu Patel and Kalinowski argue cutting them leaves no future seniors. Cat Wu and Karri Saarinen hire engineers with product taste in place of extra PMs.

## 3. Function playbooks

**Pioneer sellers before a VP of Sales** (Pete Kazanjy, Pete Kazanjy) — Close 10-30 deals yourself, hire two early-stage sellers from an adjacent company's early team, hire a leader later. Related benchmarks: Jen Abel says first seller at about $1M ARR and 7-10 customers, hire two because about half fail, 50/50 base/OTE and roughly 8-12% commission, founder joins the first five calls. Jason Lemkin: two reps hitting quota before a VP; first VP should be a stretch hire; the VP carries a bag 20-30 hours a week early on; hire someone whose last product was harder to sell.

**Would you buy it from them** (Jason Lemkin, Jason M Lemkin) — Interview about 30 reps; hire people you would buy your product from; ignore logos; make finalists demo your product for real with prep time. Colombo question: ask what they'd do in their first 14 days; if it isn't 'meet customers', out. Jen Abel's variant: hire sellers who can cosplay the founder from $1M to $10M.

**Rep ramp leading indicators** (Pete Kazanjy) — Month 1 onboarding; month 2 about 10-20 first meetings with about 50% going to second meetings; month 3 proposals; months 4-5 closes. Before blaming the rep, check you gave the deck, written discovery questions and demo script. No first meetings in a month is a quick fail signal.

**Pi-shaped marketer** (Emily Kramer, Emily Kramer) — First marketers deep in one of product marketing, content/community or growth/demand and proficient in a second; fill gaps with contractors. Hire around Series A, after some semblance of PMF; PLG needs one earlier. Business-model experience beats industry experience; avoid super-senior public-company-only candidates; test writing; ask what the candidate thinks a product marketer is and what they would do if they stepped on the gas. Related: T-shaped marketer (Adam Grenier), Arielle Jackson's trigger of about 10 people, Krithika Shankarraman's pillar-by-diagnostic.

**Growth Competency Model** (Adam Fishman, Adam Fishman) — Four buckets (growth execution, customer knowledge, growth strategy, communication and influence), three skills each; hire to balance the team. For a first junior growth hire prioritize execution and customer knowledge; consider internal hires. Painter, architect, surgeon: don't make a specialist the first hire. Also: Melissa Tan hires attributes (analytical, creative, first-principles) over channel expertise; Hila Qu says a data analyst may be the first hire and the growth PM should map to your starting point; Casey Winters says hire growth only after you've unlocked a scalable channel.

**First paid hires** (Timothy Davis; Jonathan Becker) — Data judgment over platform skills (signal vs noise); order: data-driven operator, creative, then a dedicated data scientist; set a spend milestone to move from agency to in-house.

**First product ops hire** (Melissa Perri, Melissa Perri + Denise Tilles) — Match to the pillar: data (analyst/consultant with BI storytelling, not a database engineer), process (product background; not agile coaches who never did product), research (user research or research ops). Hire experienced if you lack coaching time. Screen with 'a process you hated and automated'.

**Researcher and designer hiring** (Judd Antin; Katie Dill; Evan Spiegel; Julie Zhuo) — Antin: five tools of a researcher and a Swiss army knife interview (multiple methods across a day, week, month). Dill: taste and character over tools. Spiegel: portfolio-only, wide range, story behind a piece. Zhuo: show you care about design to hire designers.

## 4. Sourcing and outreach

**Treat every hire like an executive search** (Adam Ward) — Scope precisely, map the roughly 50 best people, pursue them over weeks, months or years, and use assessment to validate, not discover. Applies where talent is genuinely priority one; fails for mass volume or when leaders won't invest time. Anti-pattern: the funnel of doom.

**Specific-trait referral question, characteristic-based company lists, network mining sessions, hiring ideas Slack channel** (Adam Ward) — Ask trusted people who stands out on a named trait; Boolean/AI search of job posts at about 100 companies for the traits you value; sit with an employee every other week for about six weeks; a channel for non-recruiters to drop great people.

**Keep the next conversation going / warm intro first** (Adam Ward) — When timing is bad, ask for the next conversation; plant many seeds; make the first outreach land through the right person. Related: Ayo Omojola's 'you pick the people, they pick when', Luc Levesque's persistence, David Singleton's patience with critical roles.

**No-agenda courtship and market pulse** (Lauren Ipsen) — Keep a pulse on who is thriving; court target leaders as advisers; ask investors, board members and about five trusted leaders for referrals. Fails when the target senses a disguised poach. Make yourself known and relevant, play the long game.

**Recruiter selection and timing** (Lauren Ipsen; Adam Ward) — Audition recruiters: brief them, have them recite back what they heard, ask for candidate ideas on the spot. Seed/A use informal advisors or your investor's talent team; series B bring a retained firm for key hires. Ward's first recruiter: curious, business-focused, high motor, held at 90-110% capacity. Recruiters are a confidence engine, not a decision engine.

**Hiring like marketing, sales and product** (Jason Shah) — Build a brand so candidates talk to you, listen to motivations, and iterate the role itself. Benchmark: 30-40% of a founder's time on recruiting. Gokul Rajaram: about two hours a day.

**Signs of excellence and find-assess-close** (Luc Levesque) — Past performance is the best predictor; look for repeated signs across work and life. A former boss who left and came back to poach someone is a very strong signal. Three chapters: find, assess, close. Don't hire growth people on public halo.

**Missionaries and mission-driven private motivation** (Grant Lee; Nabeel Qureshi) — Authentic mission attracts missionaries; people with a personal stake in the problem work harder; undervalued talent pools (such as veterans in the 2010s) are arbitrage until copied.

## 5. Interviewing and assessment

**Structured interview before intuition** (Annie Duke, Annie Duke, from Kahneman) — Turn the implicit model of a great hire into an explicit rubric, build a structured interview from it, use intuition only after. Kahneman's work raised hit rate from about 50% to 65%. Contrast: Matt MacInnis says experienced interviewers should trust intuition but decode it with SPOTAC (smart, passionate, optimistic, tenacious, adaptable, kind). Claire Hughes Johnson: if talent is everything, your hiring process is everything; build rubrics, question banks and training. David Singleton: same loop for every candidate.

**Home court, away court, neutral court** (Shishir Mehrotra, Shishir Mehrotra) — Home court tests agreement with you; away court (their past) cannot be verified; neutral court (unrelated scenarios) is mostly what you should ask.

**Eigenquestions teleporter interview** (Shishir Mehrotra) — Give a made-up product, let the candidate ask many questions, then cap at two before they must present a plan. Judge whether the two questions split the problem into meaningful quadrants. Red flag: questions the candidate can't connect to a decision.

**Signal ranking and candidate presentation** (Shishir Mehrotra) — Weight references first, real work product and presentation second, interviews last. Presentation: half free choice, half your prompt, framed as upper-bounding (interviewers try to lower-bound).

**Work sample / onsite as two-way street** (Adam Ward, Adam Ward) — Multi-hour or day-long side-by-side projects with a choice of problems, curated greeters and meals; Ward notes Cursor removed its work trial for cost, lost confidence, and is bringing it back. Michael Truell's two-day mini project; Karri Saarinen's paid trial on a vague problem; Jason Fried's paid week plus on-the-spot riff; Nir Eyal's paid small project; Farhan Thawar's 30-90 day trial (attrition before 90 days about 20%, under 1% after) and large intern program; Merci Grace's three-hour take-home with a choice of three problems (fails: candidates may push back on unpaid work); Elena Verna's paid multi-day trial. Costly and not always feasible at high volume.

**PM loop with prep-call feedback** (Melissa Tan, Melissa Tan) — Hiring-manager screen with live problem solving on the real product, competency panels, then a presentation with a prep call where you give feedback and watch whether they incorporate it.

**Scenario and case interviews** (Casey Winters; Jeremy Henrickson; Jessica Lachs; Matt MacInnis; Tom Verrilli; Tim Holley) — Real scenarios from the role; cases too large to finish plus a changed assumption; a planted flaw to see if they notice; the same impossible case at every level with a level-calibrated rubric; a hands-on case with data defended verbally. Watch how they react when told they're wrong.

**Behavioral question families** — Ownership and drive (Upasna Gautam, Nabeel Qureshi, Geoff Charles, Joe Hudson's six levels down); self-awareness (Boz, Chris Miller, Meltem Kuran Berkowitz siblings question, Scott Belsky limitation and luck questions, Eeke de Milliano's no-luck success question, Ben Williams' fast-forward three years); failure and reflection (Maggie Crowley, Ayo Omojola, Peter Deng's most painful mistake, Denise Tilles' changed-my-mind question); communication (Judd Antin, Lane Shackleton, Laura Modi, Ryan Salva's one-minute teach-me); curiosity and agency (Josh Miller's ask-me-anything, Jessica Livingston's cues, Jason Feifer's drill-down and throw-a-theory); Tom Conrad's behavioral-only format and great-day question; Brian Halligan's board-deck exercise for execs (flattery is a red flag); David Singleton's admired-leader feedback question; Phyl Terry's ask about a failed senior hire when you are the candidate.

**Open-tools AI interviews** (Brendan Foody; Kunal Shah; Varun Mohan; Dhanji Prasanna) — Allow AI and judge what they build in an hour; Shah suggests tasks only doable with AI. Mohan and Prasanna keep an unaided onsite reasoning check and weigh critical thinking above tool fluency.

**Written job simulation screen** (Pete Kazanjy) — A Google Doc with about a dozen biographical questions; 57% don't complete it; reveals seriousness, structure and attention to detail. Related: April Dunford's persuasive writing test for marketers.

**Culture and values filters** (Patrick Campbell; Keith Yandell; Will Larson; Josh Miller; Jonathan Lowenhar) — Front-load how you handle conflict and trade-offs; a value is useful only if it excludes someone; give direct tough feedback after the interview to see how a candidate takes it; separate culture, functional and technical interviews with value ambassadors; anti-pitch interviews.

## 6. References

**Backchannel and hard reference questions** (Lauren Ipsen, Lauren Ipsen) — Backchannel references outrank provided ones; ask why you would not hire, biggest weaknesses, would you hire again, report to them, endorse them. Silence on achievements is negative. Fails when sources are themselves unvetted or biased.

**Ruthless referencing** (Keith Rabois, Keith Rabois) — About 20 references for senior hires; continue until you hit a negative one; reframe the question around the capability needed. Less useful for new grads.

**Blind and persona references** (Shishir Mehrotra) — The reference-check guide is the most important guide to write; don't name the candidate or reveal what you value; give four neutral personas; run references early. David Singleton: a loop gives about eight hours of signal, references thousands of hours.

**Rehire and performance-review questions** (Brian Halligan; Paul Adams; Rachel Lockett; Uri Levine) — Would you enthusiastically rehire; 1-10; top 1% or top 10%; what feedback will I give in their first review; would you hire them (binary). Phyl Terry's call-back-only-if-it-would-be-a-mistake trick.

## 7. Decide, close, onboard, review

**Hiring manager decides** (Adam Ward) — No hiring committees; debrief same or next day; founders stamp. Bill Carr's Bar Raiser (one trained non-manager interviewer who runs the debrief against objective criteria; time sink up to 10 hours a week; pilot in one department).

**Closing as a team sport** (Adam Ward) — Daily 10-minute standups, a Slack channel per candidate, personalized touches, slow to start and fast to close, every candidate a net promoter. Also: involve execs and family (Luc Levesque), fight to all ends (Scott Wu), ask how they will choose between offers (Will Larson), be flexible on call times (Kevin Yien), and send a how-to-work-with-me doc (Keith Yandell).

**Unsell email** (Kevin Yien, Kevin Yien) — At offer stage, no more than eight bullets of the hard truths and fears you heard; invite follow-up; accept losing mismatches (about 30% initially); send at offer, not at the start. Fails at very large scale or where recruiters are measured on offer-close rates.

**Offer-accept pre-boarding** (Adam Ward) — Dinners, early laptop, regular touchpoints through the start date.

**Review loops** (Keith Rabois; Uri Levine; Kevin Yien; Peter Deng) — 30-day would-you-make-the-same-decision (Levine: if no, act immediately; if yes, reward with equity); 6/12/18-month review against scorecards; Deng's 'in six months I'm not telling you what to do' bar. Andrew Wilkinson's fire-on-first-doubt heuristic and Brian Halligan's hire slow, fire fast are the aggressive end; Molly Graham: the best hirers are right about half the time.

**Relationship design conversation** (Kenneth Berger) — Founders hold an explicit conversation with each of the first 10-20 hires about expectations and how to work together.
