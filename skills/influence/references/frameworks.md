# Influence: Frameworks and Methods

Every distinct framework from the 328-insight digest, merged where several guests endorse the same move. Grouped by theme.

## 1. Mindset and power

**Influence vs politics** (Jessica Fain, Jessica Fain episode) — Politics manipulates outcomes and people for your own gain; influence raises the odds your good ideas survive. Run stakeholder conversations as discovery interviews. Applies to anyone uncomfortable with politics who still needs buy-in. Fails if you do not respect the exec's expertise (she says leave). Echoed by Anuj Rathi (PMs are full-stack influencers) and Ben Horowitz (influence without authority is the essence of leadership).

**Power is a tool; critical-path discipline** (Jeffrey Pfeffer, Jeffrey Pfeffer episode) — Power is neutral, like a hammer; good people need more of it if it is to be used for good. Steps: identify who is on your critical path; withhold judgment about whether you like them; do not leak disrespect through behavior. Senior executives hide what they think of you, but your collaboration odds still drop to near zero if you leak disrespect. Related Pfeffer moves: use power to get more power (deliver results, then accept bigger asks); reduce friction by doing the relational and political tasks you avoid (Laura Esserman example); success excuses most past flaws; change against entrenched interests requires power.

**Competence and respect over being liked** (Jeffrey Pfeffer) — Do not gratuitously be disliked, but do not optimize for being liked; withholding skills for fear of judgment harms you and the organization. Fails if you violate the no-asshole rule gratuitously. Contrast: Evan LaPointe and Shaun Clowes weight trust and appeal more heavily.

**Ability, Trust, Appeal** (Evan LaPointe) — Professional relationships have three parts: ability, trust (with a safety-system veto) and appeal (the experience of being around you). Appeal and trust matter more than ability. Steps: ask what kind of experience you are; if miserable, fix that first; check trust (do you ever hurt people or seem risky); then invest in ability.

**Trust equation** (Paige Costello) — Trust equals credibility plus reliability plus authenticity, divided by perceived self-interest. Build credibility with insight, reliability with your say-do ratio, authenticity with vulnerability; make clear you are not in it for another agenda. Related: Ian McAllister (trust is built by repeatedly setting and meeting expectations; lying, not shipping what you said, or repeating mistakes destroy it) and Shaun Clowes (people do not care what you know until they know you care).

**You are not the protagonist** (Hilary Gridley) — In an executive role, understand the CEO's vision and operationalize it; influence execution, not the five-year world view. Steps: understand what the CEO cares about; find the movable pieces where you have a unique perspective; still advocate when you believe. Fails if you fundamentally disagree with the vision (then consider leaving). Related: Tom Verrilli (a CPO competing with the CEO for vision ends in loggerheads) and Ami Vora (quarterly hot-takes document to build a complementary relationship).

**Obligation to advocate** (Hilary Gridley) — Build the argument in the format you are strongest at, with qualitative and quantitative data, and advocate for what you believe is true. Only after doing it well and losing should you update.

## 2. Mapping and learning stakeholders

**Stakeholder map with allies and blockers / positive politics** (Phyl Terry) — Map allies and blockers; test a safe, approachable person first with a low-stakes question; build trust before asking a vulnerable one. Rules of asking: do your homework, ask for perspective not labor, be transparent that it is a favor, pick people whose expertise fits.

**Treat the executive as your key user** (Jessica Fain) — Apply discovery-interview habits to stakeholders: probe incentives, context and their day; fold their domain expertise back into the work. Never go in just seeking approval; that makes the exec a rubber stamp.

**Learn what matters before the pitch** (Jessica Fain) — Ask the exec's EA, chief of staff and past successful pitchers what worked and what worries them; check what the exec posted lately; train a GPT on past product-review transcripts and run your PRD through it; train a model on your own feedback history to catch your usual weaknesses.

**Office Hours** (Jessica Fain, Webflow) — Book a half hour as early as possible, even before you have a one-pager, to align on strategic direction. Treat it as a user interview about their belief system and past experience.

**Sharper questions than what's top of mind** (Jessica Fain) — Ask what the board is pushing them on, what the key inputs to their success are, and what they are scared of messing up. Then show how your proposal moves their metric, OKR or board pressure.

**Leadership interviews, the fruit story** (Chandra Janakiraman) — Interview leaders before drafting strategy: what success feels like, what failure looks like, the measure of success, guiding principles, their pet ideas. Divide the leaders across working-group members.

**Magic questions** (Hilary Gridley) — To learn how someone thinks, state a belief and ask whether they agree or whether it is right, instead of open-ended questions. With legal and compliance, propose concrete scenarios and note yes or no and why. Fails when used as a coercive way to get a yes.

**LLM model of a leader / WWXD** (Hilary Gridley, Aparna Chennapragada, Jessica Fain) — Give an LLM your notes on what a leader said and how you interpreted it. Ask for the 10 criteria they will use to assess a recommendation and the top 10 ways they will object; prepare for their three likely questions. Needs enough context on the person to be useful.

**Find what influences the CEO** (Casey Winters) — Learn which sources a founder listens to (other CEOs, operators, customers, experiments) and build that case; otherwise disagree and commit, or leave. Also: at Pinterest, Winters borrowed credibility by relaying advice from growth heads the company respected.

**Two stack levels up, two down, left and right** (Fareed Mosavat) — Understand two levels below your work (technical and operational detail), two above (your boss's and their boss's priorities, up to the board) and adjacent teams on each side. Become the person who can explain how the dots connect. Related: Mosavat's mental model of how the company works and where your work creates leverage.

**What is your manager optimizing?** (Bangaly Kaba) — Before concluding your manager is the problem, ask what they are accountable for beyond your area and why your area matters to those goals.

**Alignment and confidence with your manager** (Ravi Mehta) — Track alignment and their confidence in you. High confidence lets you push a different direction; low on both means change something.

**Learn the investor's equation** (Tom Conrad) — Board questions that seem disconnected from the product probe the optimization of the business equation. Learn unit economics and frame product decisions as impact on that equation.

## 3. Pitching and pre-wiring

**Stuart plus two more** (Jessica Fain) — When an exec dictates a design, build exactly what they asked plus two alternatives you believe in, so the three become a forum for debating trade-offs.

**All options in two days** (Jessica Fain) — After a review that misses the exec's expectations, request a follow-up within two days and return with every option considered, why each does or does not serve outcomes, and technical complexity.

**Ask for what unlocks the 10x case** (Jessica Fain) — Execs do not feel your headcount, budget and timeline boundaries. State what you can do with today's resources, what the accelerated case looks like and exactly what you need; check that acceleration serves company goals.

**Build a groundswell** (Jessica Fain) — For net-new ideas, win cross-functional advocates, prototype the messy V1 yourself, socialize it. Related: Gaurav Misra's internal virality (share a working build and let it spread from engineers up to the CEO); Mihika Kapoor's company-wide demo day.

**Ground pitches in existing beliefs** (Jessica Fain) — If an idea contradicts a strongly held belief, check once or twice and then pick another of your ideas. Best early in a relationship or when trust is low.

**70 percent doc rule** (Tara Seshan, Paige Costello) — Take the doc at about 70 percent complete to the people whose buy-in you need; polished ideas bounce off people while rough edges invite co-polishing. State exactly what is missing. Seshan adds a contrarian note: in the AI era, mocks, prototypes and A/B results are stronger than long docs.

**The meeting before the meeting** (Laura Schaffer) — Recruit a respected senior ally near the CEO to endorse the pitch before the formal planning meeting. Built from a regular voice-of-customer digest that earned trust and a quarterly forum.

**Early and repeated stakeholder partnering** (Megan Cook) — List every product, team or executive the idea affects. Meet them early when you have clarity but not a finished idea; return as designs, data and user tests develop; fold in concerns so they become advocates. Buy-in is a journey, not one perfect meeting. Related: Bob Baxley (get a few key engineers and PMs to fall in love with the idea at inception, not to approve a finished one).

**Gatekeepers, key stakeholders, roadshows** (Chandra Janakiraman) — Final 2-3 weeks of a strategy process: pre-flight with 2-3 gatekeepers one-on-one; async or group review with key stakeholders; roadshows of 8-10 people. Aim to land the plan; defend the pillars using the ranking criteria and reconsider only for a good argument. Set expectations upfront that a real strategy takes 8-12 weeks.

**MOO, Most Obvious Objection** (Wes Kao) — Before you write or say anything, spend seconds listing the most obvious objections; address them up front or reframe. Know your counterarguments as well as your arguments. Fails to catch the non-obvious ones.

**Options plus a recommendation** (Matt LeMay) — Present three options, the trade-offs, and one recommendation. A single option invites hole-poking; multiple options make each exchange sharpen them.

**Comment-only living vision doc and concentric circles** (Ebi Atawodi) — Publish workshop output as a comment-only doc so anyone feels they have a say. Evangelize in three circles: core team (all triads), then stakeholders and adjacent teams (collect objections), then leadership as high as possible. Also: hand stakeholders a 10-things template to stack rank instead of letting them present 50 slides; check problem alignment by asking all your leads for the top five problems.

**Tuning fork feedback / silent read** (Kevin Yien) — Put something concrete in front of people and have them react instead of asking what they think. For busy teams, schedule a 20-minute silent read where reviewers comment live and the author responds in real time.

**Transparent logic chain** (Maggie Crowley) — Put every fact, assumption and piece of feedback in the shared doc so disagreement shifts from I disagree with you to I disagree with this point.

**Executive reporting by finishing** (Ryan Singer) — Executives are satisfied when you repeatedly say what you would do and then show it finished; time boxes need not line up with quarters.

**Frame value per audience / mirror leadership language** (Nicole Forsgren) — Pitch developer-experience wins to developers as time saved and less toil; to leaders as dollars, velocity, revenue acceleration and recovered capacity. Pick two or three metrics that match what leadership repeats. Attribution is only correlational.

**Show, don't tell** (Megan Cook, Mihika Kapoor, Nancy Duarte) — Mock the improved experience, show the current pain and a video of a customer struggling. Duarte's contrarian story: for a $100M ask, drop the laptop, hold a simple mental model and draw at a whiteboard with eye contact. Tailor the length of the what is to the listener; detail-oriented stakeholders need a longer one.

## 4. Disagreeing well

**Curiosity over being right / pause and reframe** (Ami Vora) — Treat strong disagreement as a sign the other person holds information you lack. Notice the visceral reaction, pause until the protective response passes, reinterpret it as a chance to learn, and ask them to tell you more. Fails when the disagreement is about the goal itself; then escalate.

**Pushing back on the CEO: curiosity, then data** (Tom Verrilli) — Ask whether this is their prior and what context you lack; ask if their mind is made up; if set, do not fight; if open, bring fresh data; if there is no data on either side, defer to the CEO.

**What would have to be true for them to be right** (Hilary Gridley) — Ask what insight you are missing; complete this-makes-sense-if statements; explain their rationale to your team separately from your own opinion; still disagree candidly if you do, then give it your best shot. Obviously terrible answers still warrant pushback.

**Unstated foundational assumptions** (Tobi Lutke) — Say let's figure out why there is disagreement, avoid arguing from feeling, find the divergence point and update whichever assumption is wrong.

**Decompose the disagreement into hypotheses** (Julie Zhuo) — Learn why smart people believe it; split the proposal into separate hypotheses; find the one you disbelieve; propose the smallest test (one market before 50 states); commit to the test and tell your team it is a test.

**Find the objective, then options** (Jiaona Zhang, Anneka Gupta, Jason Shah) — Test whether you truly hold conviction; understand what the founder wants for the user and business; restate the shared goal; return with a better path and data. Drop the word pushback. Gupta adds: decide whether the pet project can make or break the company before fighting. Shah's Airbnb example reframed concierges as trip designers so the smaller scope became the on-brand version.

**Concrete artifact, bake-off or quiet experiment** (Dylan Field, Merci Grace, Itamar Gilad) — Bring an example or prototype the exec can debate; build paper or Figma versions of alternatives and compare comprehension; run a small experiment on the quiet and bring the results. Fails with unreasonable leaders who punish challenge. Related: Where's your evidence (Gilad), the confidence meter as a gentle way to say no, and Teresa Torres's flip of the small-sample objection (ask how many customers informed their own decisions last week).

**Find the kernel** (Bill Carr) — After a decision you disagree with, identify the core benefit or thinking vector behind the leader's idea and expand it into something viable.

**Clear and humble ask** (Kenneth Berger) — State what you disagree with and prefer, acknowledge it is not your call and you are one opinion, say it matters to you that they know, ask whether they will reconsider. Also his Articulate, Ask, Accept loop. Berger's contrarian point: opinions and relationships carry influence without data.

**Owner not renter pushback** (Jag Duggal) — Under pressure to scale an unproven product, say early, with clarity and data, that it will not work or should be killed; you will be blamed later anyway. Fails in cultures that do not reward candor.

**Label your confidence** (Mihika Kapoor) — Say hypothesis, medium confidence, strong view, or I defer to you so others do not silently defer.

**State your intent / seed a hypothesis** (Chris Hutchins, Anneka Gupta) — Say you care only about company success and do not need to own the idea. As a senior person, share a hypothesis tied to evidence and ask what others think, since a plain statement shuts down concerns.

**Disagreeing or misunderstanding?** (Tomer Cohen) — If it is misunderstanding, restate each other's view until each can articulate the other's; if disagreeing, stop re-explaining and argue the real point. Related: Evan LaPointe's phrase choice (I have a question, or can we pull that thread, instead of I completely disagree) and Vikrama Dhiman's three tenets (raise difficult issues without being difficult, bring up topics without drawing importance to yourself, get decisions made without making them all).

**Moderate and fast influence** (Evan LaPointe) — Choose a character (devil's advocate, caregiver, protector) with permission, then a speed: slow (let them fail), moderate (teach them something and follow up in about 30 days), or fast (ask why they believe it, to drill from behavior to belief). Fast needs high trust.

**Escalation is a tool** (Molly Graham) — When equal-power peers disagree, go up together to whoever can decide, each make the case, accept the decision.

**Reflect back, then nevertheless** (Annie Duke) — As facilitator, never say I agree; reflect each person's view and ask if you got it right. After the decision say your input was incorporated; nevertheless this is the path; do not reopen. Duke argues alignment is an illusion; surface disagreement and then decide.

## 5. Requests, prioritization and managing up

**Menu of trade-offs** (Geoff Charles) — List what you are doing and not doing and why; let the exec choose; frame it as increasing velocity. You cannot ask for velocity without empowerment, trust, less process and more focus.

**Never say yes or no** (Matt LeMay) — Show how the request maps to team goals; if it has more impact, gladly build it; if unclear, discuss adjusting the impact you seek. Execs may hold information you lack.

**Schedule syncing** (Nir Eyal) — 10 minutes Monday morning with your time-boxed calendar and a list of asks you cannot fit; ask your boss to prioritize. Replaces learn to say no.

**Make trade-offs visible** (Jessica Lachs) — Ask whether the new request is more important than your existing three, yes or no; review weekly; occasionally take a quick ask for goodwill. Requires shared goals and an operating model.

**Check the strength of the exec's ask** (Jessica Fain) — Ask how strongly they feel, how urgent it is, and whether it beats three current projects.

**Follow the exec's breadcrumbs** (Jessica Fain) — Treat offhand have-you-considered comments as cues; come back fast with a concrete first answer; follow up within days. Not every comment is a mandate; check urgency.

**Shrink the change and name the failure state** (Jessica Fain, from Switch) — Make a scary investment small, with milestones every month or less; ask which outcome the exec fears most and design the experiment around it. Give certainty on the next check-in date when you cannot give certainty on outcomes.

**Heartbeat email and cheap help** (Boz Bosworth) — Five to ten sentences: progress, blocker, plan, no response required. When blocked, draft the email or yes/no question so helping is cheap. Ask each new manager how they like to be informed. Counter-view: do not try to do everything yourself.

**Say it, do it, say you did it** (Peter Deng) — State the goal in crafted language, reaffirm progress in one-on-ones and meetings, then close the loop by announcing it is done.

**Keep your boss in the loop** (Wes Kao, Ethan Evans) — Proactively update so they never ask for status; calibrate context by how reversible the decision is; err toward over-communicating. Ask your manager what you can do to help them; commit to the next-update time and deliver it.

**Build trust with results, loop-closing and killing things** (Jessica Fain) — Act on feedback, ship fast, report results back. Killing or deprioritizing your own work, with criteria and a decision date, signals shared incentives.

## 6. Building credibility fast

**Listening tour and state of the union** (Deb Liu) — First 30 days: interview many people (she did over 60), take notes, publish challenges and wishlists, report back when you finish an item. Ask each team what you can do for them this week.

**Teach me or let me help you** (Christian Idiodi) — Find the most influential person who knows the business, get your manager's permission, ask them to teach you or offer to help; they extend trust and become accountable for your growth.

**Bring the insight** (Paige Costello) — Know your customer, market, competitors, numbers and product; bring insight to every meeting instead of pretending to experience you lack.

**Voice-of-customer digest** (Laura Schaffer) — Start sharing customer pain points without asking permission; demand grows into a quarterly forum with the CEO. Related: customer advisory council verbatims (Ada Chen Rekhi), customer immersion (Chip Conley), and sales as your biggest asset for customer closeness (Annie Pearl).

**Weekly note behind the note** (Hilary Gridley) — Post a Slack rundown of the most interesting leadership conversations: what they said verbatim, your interpretation, why, and what you will do differently. Look for patterns across reviews.

**Borrow outside credibility** (Casey Winters, Brandon Chu) — Relay advice from leaders the company respects; or write externally and let outside traction raise internal credibility.

## 7. Specific stakeholders

**Engineering partners** (Camille Fournier, Will Larson, Tamar Yehoshua) — Four things that annoy engineers: hoarding credit, dismissing technical details, playing telephone, hoarding ideas. Stop being the middle layer when you say let me get back to you often. There are no villains in the workplace, only complex incentives; engineering managers must give their team interesting work. Divide ownership with your eng partner explicitly and never play one against the other.

**Sales and go-to-market** (April Dunford, Jason Lemkin, Melissa Perri) — Build the pitch with sales or it will not stick; run a cross-functional positioning workshop; push roadmap requests up to VPs rather than letting juniors trade; close the loop to sales on how their feedback is used.

**Legal and regulatory** (Keith Yandell, Laura Modi, Ryan Salva) — Learn the law until you understand it nearly as well as the lawyers, then push on conservatism; engage legal early and often; Modi's secret-shopping program turned claim review into a monthly ritual.

**Founder-CEO relationships** (Anneka Gupta, Noah Weiss, Sachin Monga, Tom Verrilli, Chip Conley) — Use the founder's power as a lever by making them an ally; involve a product-minded founder heavily at the start, step back, and bring them back to taste the real software (U-curve); start as facilitator closing the context gap; open founder-mode reviews by stating the intention and success criteria; avoid the two-dads problem. Fain's build-trust-with-results applies as well.

**Product reviews that calibrate principles** (Ami Vora, Varun Parmar) — Bring one decision and walk out with decision principles; keep reviews small and cross-functional; tier work by complexity so only big work goes to formal review.

**Meeting hygiene** (Naomi Gleit, Anneka Gupta, Bangaly Kaba, Lane Shackleton, Melissa Tan) — Agenda and pre-read 24 hours ahead and notes within 24 hours after via the invite; summarize live; recap naming each contribution; run a weekly tag-up with the triad and stakeholders; write a DACI for cross-team work.

**Networking and etiquette** (Jeffrey Pfeffer, Sam Lessin, Phyl Terry) — Lead with generosity, act as a broker who introduces people, repeat names, respect assistants and gatekeepers, do the scheduling work for busier people, send short thank-yous, give a give-to-get in conversation.
