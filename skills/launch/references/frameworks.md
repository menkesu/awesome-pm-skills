# Launch frameworks

Every framework below comes from a Lenny's Podcast guest. Where several guests endorse the same play, they are listed together. Media policies change fast, so recheck an outlet's current rules before pitching.

## A. Goal, size and readiness

**1. Define why you are launching** (Ryan Hoover, Ryan Hoover episode) — Decide the real goal before the plan. Options: customer acquisition, recruiting, fundraising momentum, feedback, partnerships, team morale, SEO. Steps: ask the goal, pick one, shape story and channel to it, use the launch as a team celebration. Fails when the goal is likes or upvotes. Related: Ryan Hoover also notes launch results are shaped by zeitgeist and by pre-launch clarity on who it is for.

**2. Know what the press is for** (Jason Feifer, Jason Feifer episode) — Only chase press when you can name its business purpose, as with raising money. Valid goals: awareness, reaching a new market, repositioning, making an exec an authority, reaching investors or partners. If you cannot give a tactical answer, postpone. Echoed by Zoelle Egner (PR is for credibility: use it in hiring outreach and cold outbound, and measure response rate with and without) and Emilie Gerber (second-order effects: third-party validation for AE emails, recruiter outreach, sponsor decks).

**3. Lightning strikes vs peanut butter** (Christopher Lochhead, Christopher Lochhead episode) — Concentrate effort into a few huge launches aimed at the ideal customer instead of even spend by quarter. Steps: define ideal customer and where they hang out, craft something provocative, be undeniable for a day or two. Cadence: 1-2 a year in B2B, 2-3 in B2C, one a quarter only if big; keep baseline marketing. Fails past a few a year.

**4. Series of launches** (Zoelle Egner, Zoelle Egner episode) — Big annual launch plus another every couple of months; novelty keeps you top of mind in communities. Early-stage default.

**5. Great enough, not good enough** (Jag Duggal, Jag Duggal episode) — In launch reviews ask whether it is great enough. If the Sean Ellis score is borderline (~50%), list the 3-5 things needed to get decisively past it and study which cohorts already exceed 50%. For tentpole products and anyone avoiding heavy paid marketing.

**6. Maximum launchable product** (Mark Pincus, Mark Pincus episode) — Separate a learning probe from a real launch; viable breeds hope. Related: Cameron Adams (Canva) held the launch for a year until the experience was joyful, launching with known rough edges outweighed by joy; works for deep-domain founders, not unknown problem spaces.

**7. Launch-readiness signals** (Claire Butler, Figma; David Singleton; Kevin Weil) — Qualitative demand beats metrics early: people take the laptop out of the demoer's hands, a few teams use it full-time, the alpha group is very happy, internal usage explodes (strong for social products, weak evidence for B2B or niche). Claire Butler also warns stealth too long demotivates the team.

**8. Winning tactic into winning strategy** (Marc Benioff, Marc Benioff episode) — Run many launch tactics in parallel, watch which breaks through, then scale it. Pair with one focal product. Related: make the flagship event about a single product.

**9. Six-point product launch plan** (Marc Benioff) — Focus the company, find more fuel, add distribution capacity, tell customer stories starting with customer zero, activate the ecosystem, motivate the installed base to turn it on. Fits enterprise with a large base.

**10. Customer zero** (Marc Benioff) — Move your own operations onto the product and use the measured result as the launch story (he cites a 50% drop in human escalation and 83% of inquiries resolved by the agent).

**11. Delay awareness in early B2B** (Meltem Kuran Berkowitz, Meltem Kuran episode) — Harvest people ready to convert for 6-8 months, prove messaging, then run awareness. Consumer founders can skip this.

## B. Staged rollout and AI launches

**12. Research preview / iterative deployment / release earlier** (Cat Wu; Kevin Weil; Boris Cherny; Jenny Wen; Tara Seshan) — For AI products where nobody knows the full capability surface, brand the release as early, ship in a week or two, iterate in public, kill what fails. Frame: this is the worst it will ever be. Fails: high-stakes or regulated settings; creates overlapping features and needs extra education.

**13. Safety funnel** (Tanguy Crusson, Tanguy Crusson episode) — Hard-cap how many customers see an unfinished product, work with them until it is amazing, then invite more. Protects customers you cannot win back; counters MAU-driven pressure to go wide. Related: Benjamin Mann on Anthropic holding back a consumer computer-use app until a trust bar was met.

**14. Opt-in labs, technical preview, small-market soft launch** (Robby Stein; Ryan J. Salva; Peter Deng) — Get real usage before full launch. Stein: opt-in Labs shows real query data. Salva: grow a technical preview from tens to hundreds of thousands and treat organic excitement as the signal to scale hiring. Deng: Instagram's Bolt tested in small markets to read retention.

**15. Launch to everyone, no waitlist** (Nick Turley, Nick Turley episode) — Lets you watch emergent use cases live. Contrarian; needs distribution and capacity to absorb load. Pair with exposing affordances, since horizontal products leave users with an empty box.

**16. Layered defaults rollout** (Stewart Butterfield, Stewart Butterfield episode) — For behavior-changing features at scale: notify admins weeks ahead, set a sensible org default, admins can override, users can override, later admin changes re-override. Ship a default or most people never turn it on.

**17. Build with the community / Product Lab** (Sachin Monga, Sachin Monga episode) — Mock up for ~10 users, pilot, keep an invite-only lab of ~100 power users, nothing rolled out to all first. Expect v1 to differ from the plan. Best where changes affect livelihoods.

**18. Soft launch vs hard launch** (Janna Bastow, Janna Bastow episode) — Engineering ships on its own date (soft launch); marketing runs a hard launch from a working product with videos and beta testimonials; launch-readiness meetings show sales what is in the now column. Hard-dated launches (regulatory, seasonal) need an early soft launch.

**19. Evergreen launch room** (Cat Wu) — Standing channel where engineers post dogfooded features; docs, PMM and DevRel ship announcements the next day. The PM sets up the process. For teams shipping weekly with small partner teams.

**20. Pre-plan the leak** (Adam Mosseri, Adam Mosseri episode) — For any controversial test or change: hold a comms discussion up front, assume it leaks, decide proactive vs reactive and the message. Related: Ethan Evans argues for broad betas over surprise launches even if some testers leak.

**21. Breaking-news dress rehearsal** (Upasna Gautam, Upasna Gautam episode) — Script an end-to-end simulation of the highest-stakes event, with engineers and support observing, to find what breaks. For products with a peak-usage moment.

## C. Cross-functional readiness

**22. Knowing it is coming vs knowing what to do with it** (Christine Itwaru, Christine Itwaru episode) — After a bad launch, Pendo ran a company-wide retrospective and a product digest for revenue teams on how to get ready and the added value, without teaching them to sell.

**23. PM-to-marketing brief loop** (Emily Kramer, Emily Kramer episode) — PM shares what is being built; marketing returns a brief on audience, channels, top-line messaging; PM weighs in before content is written. Related: Nan Yu on an embedded PMM owning release language with sales testing words in the field, and PMs originating customer language; Denise Tilles on product ops owning a GTM template and cadence.

**24. Launch is not complete at code complete** (Krithika Shankarraman, Krithika Shankarraman episode) — Audit shipped-but-unannounced features, define complete as customer awareness and usage, expand beyond a blog post. Also her DATE framework: diagnose, analyze, take a different path, experiment; and warning against copying playbook outputs without their inputs.

## D. Story and messaging

**25. Second-grader test** (Lulu Cheng Meservey, Lulu Cheng Meservey episode) — Reduce to one sentence a second-grader could understand, strip cliches, convert to an analogy or image. Inside jokes and esoteric references fall flat. Companion: make ideas spread by turning them into a joke, analogy, mental image or story, and giving people a personal reason to repeat them.

**26. Cultural erogenous zones** (Lulu Cheng Meservey) — Shape the message to the audience's existing passions; build a bridge from X (what they care about) to Y (your offer). Accept that some people are not your audience. Fails with no natural fit.

**27. Story before product / press release first** (Brian Chesky; Tony Fadell; Jeetu Patel) — Work out the story months ahead; it often dictates the product; each release is an episode in a longer story. Fadell: write the press release before the project, limit to 3-4 tentpole features, and cut anything that does not help sell it. Patel: story first, product supplies proof.

**28. Five-second moment and start at the end** (Matthew Dicks, Matthew Dicks episode) — Every story is built around one tiny moment of change; identify it, define its opposite as the start state, open as close to it as possible. Related Dicks plays: speaking with adjacency (match theme, not content), inserting a 30-second personal anecdote into a corporate narrative, bricks vs band-aids (build a story vault in advance), Homework for Life (log one moment a day; ~10% become stories), personal interest inventory. Lane Shackleton also points to Storyworthy.

**29. Hero's journey and movements** (Mike Maples Jr., Mike Maples Jr. episode) — Describe the world that is, issue a call to adventure, honor resistance, give a credible tool, make the early believer the hero and the founder Obi-Wan. Early customers are moved by belief, not pragmatism; appeal to a higher purpose instead of being better than a competitor. Do not rehost one pitch for press, investors and employees.

**30. Name the object of the new game** (Andy Raskin, Andy Raskin episode) — Boil the narrative into the goal of the new game, often phrased as a question; use as website copy and mission.

**31. Why, not what; meet them in context; truth** (Tony Fadell) — Technology-led teams talk about what; customers need why. Place the product in the customer's world; change messaging by adopter wave and geography; borrow infomercial structure (doubt, pain, easy answer) with honest claims.

**32. Plain words tests** (Max Schoening; Ryan Hoover; Uri Levine; Gina Gotthilf; Christian Idiodi; Sean Ellis) — Whiteboard test; describe it the way customers or friends do; open with problem or user value; ask whether another company could have written the copy; use references' exact words; mine written 'why' answers for hooks. Raaz Herzberg adds that product people live in gray areas but messages must be black and white to survive scaling.

**33. Languaging and remarkable products** (Christopher Lochhead; Seth Godin) — New language drives new thinking; do not describe a new thing with old words, but anchor to familiar ones. Godin: remarkable means worth remarking on; decide what you want people to say; a brand is a promise; buzz is not traction.

## E. Audiences and going direct

**34. Concentric circles and the one-page comms plan** (Lulu Cheng Meservey) — 1-3 message points, then convince co-founders, execs, employees and investors, then power users and outward. List 5-6 audiences ranked by influence and credibility, with what they care about, where they get information, message and channel. A one-page doc solves most comms problems.

**35. Going direct** (Lulu Cheng Meservey) — A senior person, authentic, first person, on the one medium they do best; content prepared before launch; consistent, not viral. Defensive (audience primed before an attack) and offensive (only you tell your story). Fails when ghostwritten or spread across six channels. Echoed by Elena Verna (social is the new organic: CEO and team posting), Emilie Gerber (invest in an exec with bold opinions; test for a month or two), Grant Lee (founder-led marketing, tailor per platform, bank goodwill), Camille Ricketts (no posting quota). Counter: Qasar Younis says founders with a network can stay quiet; Camille Ricketts says earned press still matters.

**36. Launch-day assets by community** (Ryan Hoover; Drew Houston; Karri Saarinen; Grant Lee; Eric Simons) — Product Hunt: human tagline, short maker comment, gallery as slideshow. Dropbox: meme-laden demo video grew the waitlist from 5,000 to 85,000 (Houston says that era is over). Saarinen: write directly in the first audience's language, each founder posts their reasons. Gamma: a provocative launch tweet. Bolt: a single tweet; watch days 2-3 before celebrating.

**37. Content routines** (Grant Lee; Ada Chen Rekhi; Sriram Krishnan; Julian Shapiro; Gergely Orosz) — Notepad-to-post weekly; LinkedIn 30; publish daily for reps; novelty idea bank scored 0-5. Orosz: years of accidental work sit behind overnight stories. Wes Kao: Seth Godin's blog works because of insight density, not the daily format.

## F. Press craft

**38. Three steps: prep, who, pitch** (Jason Feifer) — Prep: decide what press is for and how to tell the story. Who: specific writers and editors receptive to you. Pitch: short, customized message built from the first two.

**39. Decode the outlet** (Jason Feifer) — Infer the unwritten mission by reading 5+ stories; parse by desk for big outlets; reverse-engineer formats for your category (roundups, seasonal lists); click bylines; audit competitor coverage; slice your story into facets and assign one per outlet.

**40. Pitch the writer, and freelancers** (Jason Feifer) — Do not email the visible top editor; staff vs writer bios; Google freelancers' portfolio sites. Contrarian: freelancers read and act more.

**41. Publication-to-story matching** (Emilie Gerber, Emilie Gerber episode) — TechCrunch funding and product (weak in health tech and MarTech), Axios deals only, Business Insider decks and journeys, VentureBeat AI, Fast Company future of work and design, Forbes awards and contributor network. WSJ, NYT and CNBC features need valuation and revenue on record. TechCrunch wrote about 9 funding stories in a week when about 50 rounds happened.

**42. Exclusives, staggered** (Emilie Gerber; Jason Feifer; Arielle Jackson) — Offer exclusives to 1-6 reporters in sequence; move on after about a week; follow up after 3-4 days with new info. Feifer: be transparent, use a fixed head start window or an exclusive interview, never play outlets against each other. Jackson: early-stage launches are single-outlet exclusives and you cannot dictate the date; bundle funding with a bigger story. Gerber: optimize for the reporter, not the date.

**43. Straightforward funding pitch** (Emilie Gerber) — Say it is a launch or funding exclusive with amount, space, investors, to the beat reporter. Do not convolute the message.

**44. Glance-test pitch email** (Jason Feifer; Emilie Gerber) — Targeted subject and first sentence, genuine references, three short paragraphs, story relevant to the audience first. Include the column name and suggested guest in the subject. Mirror the show's format (the Problem Solvers Show example). Pre-warm the writer on social. Point out a gap in their coverage. Do not call.

**45. Hooks without hard news** (Gerber; Feifer; Egner; Jackson) — Counter-consensus take (Gerber: reporters have nine pitches with the same stance; Clockwise op-ed on Shopify meetings). Underdog framing (Ramp vs Bill.com). Publish proprietary data (Feifer: Corcoran Report). Be part of the story. Expert-source positioning. Pick the most interesting thread (Column Tax). Personal angle. Newsjacking with a safe stance. Local customers as heroes (Jackson). Bigger-than-you point of view (Egner). Problem-solving stories beat success stories (Feifer).

**46. Assets: blog post over wire release** (Emilie Gerber; Jason Feifer) — Wire releases cost $300-2,000 and nobody reads them. Publish a first-person post with all news elements, own graphics, social, your own site. Gamma-style launch page for visual products. Absolute figures, not tiny-base percentages. Nothing materially detailed in email before interest, because it is on the record. Public companies and regulated news are the exception.

**47. Podcasts and newsletters** (Emilie Gerber) — Qualify via search, host history, activity, past guests; match spokesperson role to the show; open-the-door note; pitch what the audience wants (tactical lessons vs launches). Morning Brew Coworking forms (~50% success reported), top-startup lists, joint conference panels. Expect ~1 opportunity per 20-30 sends. Sri Batchu: newsletters and podcasts reach tech founders and help recruiting; traditional PR reaches the non-tech majority.

**48. Press as a prize** (Jason Feifer) — The reusable social proof matters more than readership: as-seen-in badges, paid promotion to the audience you want, links in sales and recruiter emails.

**49. Choosing a PR agency** (Emilie Gerber; Jason Feifer) — Do not buy on big logos; ask what month one looks like; read recent writing and wins; meet the day-to-day team; skip extensive RFPs. Prefer agencies that push back on you and do not guarantee placement or sell distribution. Budgets: boutiques from about $10K a month, consultants about $8K, up to roughly $30K for Series C, projects $10-20K.

**50. Brand and naming adjuncts for launches** (Arielle Jackson; David Placek) — Get the story, positioning and name settled before pitching (Jackson); naming brief, seven criteria, 3-5 finalists (Jackson); polarization as a signal of a strong name (Placek). Use only if the launch includes naming.
