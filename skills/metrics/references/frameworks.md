# Metrics frameworks (north star, metric tree, guardrails, instrumentation, cadence)

Each entry: originator (episode) - what it is for; when it applies or fails; steps; benchmarks. Where several guests endorse the same idea they are merged and named.

## 1. Choosing the north star

**Hierarchy of Engagement, level 1: the core action** (Sarah Tavel, Sarah Tavel episode) - Replace sign-ups, downloads and MAU with the single action that proves the user understood the product and will return. Applies: consumer products, strongest in consumer social. Steps: define the one core action (friending at Facebook, pinning at Pinterest, writing a note at Evernote) -> track the share of weekly cohorts completing it -> point every roadmap item and the new-user experience at it. Fails: products where the buyer is not the user.

**Bottoms-up / top-down core action analysis** (Sarah Tavel) - How to find the core action. Bottoms-up: for every action compute % of users who complete it and the probability a user who did it this week returns next week; rank. Top-down: what is the product for, and has a user who never does the action understood it? Pinterest chose weekly active pinners (pinners returned more than 90% of the time). Judge every experiment by whether the share of pinners or pins per cohort rises. Applies: early-stage consumer. Caveat: it is a best bet, so re-derive as the product matures (YouTube moved from watching to subscribing). Good core actions help both sides of a network.

**Happy GMV** (Sarah Tavel) - For marketplaces, track GMV generated by transactions that make buyers or sellers happy enough to retain; customers compare you with substitutes, not with your size. Steps: define the happy-path experience a side needs in order to retain -> track retention after a transaction rather than raw GMV -> count growth only where it feeds that path. Also warns that chasing the $1M GMV Series A milestone diffuses founders across cities or categories.

**North Star metric picking checklist** (Sean Ellis, Sean Ellis episode) - Derive the north star from the must-have value (PMF survey), not revenue. Rules: counts units of value delivered, not a ratio, can go up and to the right, correlates with revenue without being revenue. Add a time window (weekly rides, monthly purchases). Time-box to about 30 minutes with the right raw ingredients. A $1,000 and a $10 purchase deliver the same 'I needed something' value. Endorsed in spirit by Itamar Gilad and Tavel.

**Value-exchange loop** (Itamar Gilad, Itamar Gilad episode) - Organizations deliver value to the market and capture value back; measure both. North star = value created for the market (WhatsApp messages sent, Airbnb nights booked); top KPI = value captured (revenue or profit). Build a tree for each and look for overlapping middle metrics. Applies beyond PMF; may be overkill for early-stage companies that do not yet know how they create value.

**Activation metric from the retention curve** (Naomi Gleit, Facebook; Adriel Frederick; Noah Weiss, Slack; Lauryn Isford, Airtable) - Find the early behavior that predicts retention or upgrade, set a low-bar critical-mass threshold, and rally every team on it. Facebook: 10 friends in 14 days (value was the discrete goal with a deadline, not the exact number; artifact risk: a retention dip near 11 to 12 friends from a code limit). Slack: teams of five people using Slack for most of the work week were 400% more likely to upgrade within six months. Airtable: workspace-level activation for seat-based horizontal products. Gleit: most of the value is alignment, so do not stall hunting for the perfect regression. Isford: a lower activation rate (5 to 15%) beats an easy milestone.

**Build the north star from the strategy** (Lauryn Isford) - Draw the funnel, find the biggest opportunity, build a metric that reflects the output of that strategy. A north star measures whether the chosen strategy is delivering, not dictates it. Keep it stable at least six months for momentum, domain expertise and compounding, then change when you outgrow it; the same metric forever probably hides a bigger opportunity.

**Activation metric family** (Lauryn Isford) - Pair the team activation north star with individual week-2 and week-4 retention and a build-sophistication score so onboarding changes cannot win on one dimension and hurt others.

**Candidate actions scored on conversion and retention** (Hila Qu) - Measure 90-day conversion and 30-day retention for users who took each candidate action; looking at only one gives an incomplete picture.

**D5/D7 north star** (Josh Miller, Josh Miller episode) - Number of people who use the product at least five days a week; captures retention, engagement and growth in one hard-to-game number. Define activity strictly, track week-over-week growth rate, review cohort by cohort. Arc grew more than 10% weekly for about eight months with D5/D7 retention in the low-30s to low-40s percent. Fails: naturally low-frequency products.

**CURR as the retention lever on DAU** (Jackson Shuttleworth, Duolingo) - Model which input moves the north star most per percentage point (current user retention rate, i.e. non-new, non-resurrected users returning tomorrow) and aim retention teams at it. Evaluate features on more than one retention metric: two streak freezes hurt CURR but won weekly-active return rate.

**Quality metric as a balancing top-line** (Nickey Skarstad, Airbnb Experiences) - In an early marketplace product with an experience component, make a quality metric that can conflict with growth (review rate) the north star, backed by coaching for supply below the bar.

**Not one metric for every business** (Merci Grace) - Slack's needle-moving work turned out to be getting people into a new team at the same time. Run experiments and research to find what moves your needle instead of importing a universal recipe.

**Mission above metrics** (Alex Hardiman; Logan Kilpatrick; Ami Vora) - Business goals serve the mission. Map each metric to the resource it unlocks (revenue buys compute buys better models). Customer value and company value rarely diverge over a long enough horizon; put short-term divergences on a timeline.

**Share by real unit of attention** (Roger Martin) - Measure market share by the real unit of customer attention (minutes at a smart screen), not the nominal category, to catch disruption hidden in traditional share numbers.

## 2. Metric trees, inputs and goals

**Input and output metrics** (Bill Carr, Bill Carr episode) - Focus teams on controllable customer-experience inputs (selection, price, speed) rather than outputs like revenue. Steps: map the flywheel -> pick inputs you control that touch the customer -> measure each more than one way -> goal and review weekly on inputs; iterate with DMAIC. At Amazon only about 10 of roughly 500 S-team goals had a financial metric. Applies where end-of-quarter revenue fire drills dominate.

**Finding input metrics** (Bill Carr) - Map the end-to-end customer experience; instrument speed, quality and ease at each step; check you can control it and it affects the customer; observe effect on outputs.

**Metrics trees** (Itamar Gilad) - Break the north star and the top KPI into trees to estimate impact, align teams on a shared tree, assign sub-metrics to teams for ongoing ownership, and occasionally reorganize team topology around goals.

**North-star translation layer** (Sri Batchu, Instacart and Ramp) - Pick a north star growth can influence (monthly active orders; dollars of SQL pipeline). Let each team own a local metric it can move; finance and data build conversion factors from each local metric into the north star; roll up all plans in north-star units; refresh every six months. Imprecise: do not use it for marginal calls within about 5 to 10 percent.

**Volume and efficiency pair** (Sri Batchu) - Choose two success metrics, one volume (intuitive, tied to value, between too-lagging revenue and too-granular team metrics) and one efficiency. Optimizing CAC alone attracts less valuable customers; LTV:CAC is unreliable for young companies.

**Common currency for trade-offs** (Jessica Lachs, Jess Lachs episode) - Quantify price, delivery time, selection, conversion and supply in one currency (DoorDash: Gross Order Value and volume); build an inventory of options with expected return and timeframe; use it to trade off across teams.

**Goal on proxy inputs, not retention** (Jessica Lachs) - Ask what inputs drive the long-term outcome, pick short-term measurable proxies, and run experiments to test whether the proxies drive the long-term output.

**Simple beats composite** (Jessica Lachs; Bill Carr 'fitness function' critique) - Replace weighted composites with 2 to 3 understandable metrics. DoorDash merchant health score 0.35 -> new-merchant first order within seven days plus photo coverage and accurate hours (about 95% of the value; handle the last 5% later). Carr: a compound metric becomes meaningless because you cannot tell which action drove it.

**Absolute numbers over rates** (Archie Abrams) - Orient funnel teams to the total number of people who complete their stage; rate owners tend to make the preceding step harder. Expect rate to fall while total activated rises.

**Volume plus conversion guardrail** (Emily Kramer) - Top-of-funnel goal = volume plus a maintained or better downstream conversion (signups at same-or-better activation rate); watch the whole funnel.

**Cohort GMV value** (Archie Abrams) - For power-law marketplaces measure total GMV a cohort of acquired merchants generates over one to five years, convert to revenue and gross profit, compare against payback guardrails.

**Market health metric and liquidity** (Benjamin Lauzier, Lyft) - Liquidity = share of intentful demand (app opens, searches) that becomes a transaction; raw fill rate is distorted by exogenous factors. Find the predictor that plateaus and correlates with retention (Lyft: closest driver within about 2 minutes ETA) and give supply teams that proxy as the goal.

**Growth model building blocks** (Dan Hockenmaier) - SaaS: acquisition channels, retention, monetization. Transactional: add frequency, AOV, unit economics. Marketplaces: add supply acquisition and retention and side interaction (junk-in junk-out risk; check incrementality of new supply). Link paid spend to reinvested contribution margin: payback period beats LTV:CAC. Sri Batchu and Yuriy Timen agree; consumer subscription paid media should now pay back within six months, not twelve.

**Model the whole business before optimizing leaf nodes** (Tom Conrad) - Build a financial model from BI, transactions and expenses; model LTV and CAC; find the highest-leverage variables; focus the product team there rather than on arbitrary leaf-node conversion rates.

**KPI per journey milestone** (Georgiana Laudi) - Unique visitors for problem, primary-CTA conversion for interest, meaningful usage threshold of key features for first value and value realization, repeat use and expansion for growth.

**Metrics by function in PLG** (Hila Qu) - Top of funnel = high-quality signups, not traffic; product owns activation and activated teams; sales owns PQL conversion. Elena Verna's PQA signals: users (about seven is the usual enterprise threshold), product-specific volume, and velocity of change.

**Product-lens business metrics** (Melissa Perri) - Slice ARR and retention by customer segment, product line and feature adoption; test strategy claims (like going upmarket) by checking whether enterprise customers adopt and retain by feature. Jason Cohen adds: track logo count N next to NRR, because a percentage loss needs a larger percentage gain to recover (20% loss needs 25% gain), and median NRR at IPO is about 119%.

**Retention benchmarks for SMB SaaS** (Jason Cohen; Jason Lemkin) - Above about 3% monthly logo cancellation is terrible, about 2% is good for SMB; churn is the number-one PLG metric; 3 to 4% monthly SMB churn is nearly unsolvable.

## 3. Guardrails, gaming and trade-offs

**Overall Evaluation Criterion (OEC)** (Ronny Kohavi, Ronny Kohavi episode) - What you optimize must be causally predictive of long-term lifetime value, pairing the revenue goal with countervailing user-experience metrics. Steps: pick primary metric -> add countervailing metrics (retention, time to successful click, percent of successful sessions) -> estimate long-term cost of short-term gain -> express as a constraint (a fixed ad pixel budget). Test: if the team cannot agree directionally that an increase is good, redefine it (microsoft.com support: half the room read more time on site as good, half as bad). For delayed outcomes, build a predictive model or run long-term learning experiments.

**Price the harm** (Ronny Kohavi) - The email team credited for every purchase created spam; modeling the lifetime cost of an unsubscribe made over half of campaigns negative and led to per-campaign unsubscribe options. Eric Ries adds the Groupon email-frequency creep: define principles an experiment may not override and include trust guardrails.

**Guardrails for healthy usage** (Paige Costello, Asana) - Pair the headline metric (org paying weekly active users) with a definition of healthy usage so one metric is not driven at the expense of real value. Using a feature is not success: that is teaching to the test.

**Safety plus progress pair** (Shweta Shrivastava, Waymo) - Two metric families: commercial/operational (trips per week, DAU/WAU, funnel, cost to operate) and system behavior (safety, road rules, progress). Benchmark safety against human performance (collisions per 100,000 miles) and pair with progress counter-metrics.

**Fail-state goals** (Jessica Lachs) - Goal on rare disaster outcomes (orders never delivered) because averages hide them; staff a cross-functional team to eradicate them. Jeff Weinstein's 'users having a bad day' chart is the same idea: one log line per bad-day reason, stacked by frequency.

**Zero-support-ticket cohort metric** (Jeff Weinstein, Stripe Atlas) - Percentage of companies completing the whole journey with zero support tickets went from 15% to 85% in about 18 months and tracked market share. Binary per-customer, cohort percentage, tactical KRs beneath one metric. Risk: hiding support unless paired with countermeasures. Related: constrain tactics with a metric per tactic to avoid perverse incentives.

**Incentives on behavior, longer horizon** (Kristen Berman) - Behavioral tactics become manipulative or benign depending on incentives; set KPIs on the customer behavior and lengthen the horizon beyond a quarter.

**Velocity control mechanisms** (Geoff Charles) - Share every negative review monthly, report NPS, CSAT and support tickets normalized by users, assign bugs to on-call, treat red metrics as a stop on new features.

**Goodhart and the 20 percent** (Tobi Lutke) - Treat metrics as cockpit instruments, not the objective; any metric that becomes a goal stops being a good one; only about 20% of the most valuable product work is fully quantifiable.

**Customer-hostile optimization** (Kayvon Beykpour) - An aggregate metric lets teams keep experiences that are good for the metric and bad for people (a feed toggle that reverts to ranked after about a day). Ask whether it is good for the customer, not only the metric.

**Metrics follow the campaign objective** (Timothy Davis) - Conversion campaigns: conversions; awareness: reach; consideration: demos or downloads. Click share over impression share. Krithika Shankarraman and Eli Schwartz: clicks, impressions, rankings and traffic are vanity; follow lead -> sales-qualified -> pipeline -> revenue.

## 4. Insight, analysis and instrumentation

**Observation vs insight** (Crystal Widjaja, Crystal W / EOY Review) - A measurement is a data point; an insight explains why and tells you how to act. Steps: record the observation -> segment by event properties -> hypothesize the why -> test causality -> change something in the real world. Do not treat metric gathering as entertainment. Track user journeys and experiences, not KPIs.

**Event taxonomy by walking the experience** (Crystal Widjaja) - For each button ask why a user would or would not press it, and whether that is captured in a property. Symptom of bad tracking: many events with one or zero properties; log context (drivers on screen, location, surge, voucher). Widjaja's stack by stage: Data Studio for a single warehouse, Metabase for SQL skills or multiple databases, CleverTap for mobile events, then Amplitude, Segment and an experimentation tool.

**Instrumentation audit and data dictionary** (Hila Qu) - List key actions, check each is tracked correctly, reinstrument gaps, publish a dictionary. Tool choice matters less than clean data. Add a warehouse and ETL once real data users appear. Customer 360: connect usage, campaigns and CRM at the account level.

**Start small, measure to reduce uncertainty** (John Cutler) - Instrument about 20 events on a free plan, expect to wipe some; measure just enough to reduce uncertainty for the next critical decision (Hubbard). Ben Williams: collect intentionally with tracking plans and test schema conformance in CI. Manik Gupta: pick a definition of active, instrument and codify it.

**Server-side tracking by default** (Vijay Iyengar) - Events as structured logs with a user ID from your servers: 100% reach, controlled environment; client JavaScript tracking drops 20 to 30% of events and mobile clients leave stale tracking. Add client-only context later. Model every sales, marketing and product interaction as an event; the warehouse is the center of gravity.

**What data are you missing?** (Jessica Lachs) - Users who cannot log in are not in the denominator; quantify lost future orders from bad experiences.

**Averages mislead** (Jessica Lachs; Tom Verrilli) - Break averages into distributions: a referral channel was bimodal (good users plus fraud). Before deprecating a 3% feature, check whether it is 100% of someone's core use case.

**Root-cause segmentation** (Robby Stein) - When a core metric moves, cut by region, device, demographic and use case before designing the treatment. Julie Zhuo: diagnose with data, treat with design.

**Cell-by-cell funnel grid** (Mayur Kamat, Binance) - Grid of segment x variant (top 50 countries x top 10 document types, about 500 cells) with conversion in each; fix worst cells one at a time. Bangaly Kaba: map the funnel before iterating on a product you do not understand.

**Re-instrument when optimizing for the first time** (Ayo Omojola) - Re-measure, rebuild queries and visuals, look at it many ways, reconcile every disagreement; never let null carry meaning.

**Attribution** (Austin Hay, Jonathan Becker, Yuriy Timen, Ethan Smith) - Capture URL, referrer, UTMs and click IDs from day one, store first and last UTM; evolve first/last touch -> 50/50 -> multi-touch. No single source of truth: triangulate cookie data with MMM and surveys. MMM only above about $100K monthly spend and three or more channels. Browsers stripping parameters inflate organic. B2B answer-engine impact needs presence tracking and a 'how did you hear about us' question.

**Benchmarks with care** (Elena Verna; Nicole Forsgren) - Benchmarks differ in definitions; use as directional input. Freemium conversion about 5% and trial 10 to 15% in true PLG; DORA elite: on-demand deploys, under-a-day lead time, under-an-hour restore, 0 to 15% change fail (2019 numbers).

## 5. AI and engineering productivity

**SPACE** (Nicole Forsgren, Nicole Forsgren episode) - Satisfaction and wellbeing, Performance, Activity, Communication and collaboration, Efficiency and flow. Use at least three at once so they balance; survey satisfaction every few months; example: pair PR review alert count with protected coding time and a PR-process survey. Satisfaction over happiness. Survey data is trustworthy absent a hostile workplace.

**Prescriptive vs framework metrics** (Nicole Forsgren; Will Larson) - DORA four metrics are prescriptive and diagnostic (use only for pipeline speed and stability, never to judge people); SPACE is a lens. For AI add trust as a dimension. Larson: report an imperfect metric and educate stakeholders over time.

**End-to-end velocity** (Nicole Forsgren; Tomer Cohen) - Idea-to-customer or idea-to-experiment time, with openly disclosed attribution between AI and DevEx. Cohen: experiments x quality / time from idea to launch.

**Motion is not progress** (Fiona Fung, Max Schoening, Molly Graham, Inbal Shani, Chip Huyen, Forsgren) - Lines of code, PR counts, token spend and time saved are activity. Use time to value (Shani), outcomes and quality, and a listening tour with senior engineers.

**AI product metrics** (Julie Zhuo, Mike Krieger, Tomer Cohen, Asha Sharma, Roman Ugarte) - Bucket intent with an LLM; avoid engagement and likability as the north star for assistants (sycophancy); measure emotion and downstream conversation; for AI teams the KPI is how fast they ingest data and turn it into a reward model; delegation needs completion you can walk away from.

## 6. Cadence and culture

**Weekly Business Review** (Ian McAllister, Amazon) - Leaders present variances and trends for every key metric in one hour and answer probing questions; cascade it down.

**Daily numbers update** (Daniel Lereya, Monday.com) - A bot posts each team's numbers daily; anomalies are discussed; low adoption of AI features across 250,000 accounts was fixed by opening them to 98% of customers within two weeks.

**Metric hygiene and ritual** (Jeff Weinstein) - Short feeling-laden names, one fixed dashboard URL, live metric in meetings, expect weeks of definition fixes. David Singleton: a predictable review cadence ladders micro-decisions up to value. Varun Parmar: after shipping, compare actual to target.

**Mission over vanity; delight math** (Gibson Biddle; Katie Dill) - Convert a retention lift into dollars (customers saved x LTV x word-of-mouth factor) and compare with cost; collect internal examples where quality moved business metrics.

**Early-stage exceptions** (Aparna Chennapragada; Tanguy Crusson; Karri Saarinen; Bob Baxley) - Zero-to-one: qualitative signals, one or two jobs nailed, MAU expected to look low until ready; no per-feature metric targets; design cannot reliably own a number.

**Direction over threshold** (Chris Hutchins; Adriel Frederick) - Direction of later cohorts beats an absolute threshold; a slightly distorted metric is acceptable if it creates organizational momentum.

**Pulse and sentiment** (Judd Antin; Jag Duggal; Brian Halligan; Matt Mullenweg) - CSAT beats NPS on data properties; Nubank treats a one or two point NPS dip as an alarm; departmental employee NPS exposes manager problems; sentiment must be read per channel (negatives 8% on LinkedIn, Facebook and Instagram vs 52% on Twitter).
