## The survey and the 40% bar

"The question is, how would you feel if you could no longer use this product? Once you got a high enough percentage of users saying they'd be very disappointed, most of those products did pretty well."
— Sean Ellis, Lenny's Podcast (00:00:07)

"What I recommend is a random sample of people who've really used your product. So they've gone in, they didn't just sign up, but they went in and hopefully hit that deviation moment. They've used it twice, two plus times"
— Sean Ellis, Lenny's Podcast (00:22:38)

"you can kind of find out day one, you don't need a good analytics system in place to be able to see if product market fit exists."
— Sean Ellis, Lenny's Podcast (00:06:40)

"I basically at one point said I need at least 30 responses, and I just thought I randomly made up a number"
— Sean Ellis, Lenny's Podcast (00:33:11)

"the companies that struggled to grow almost always had less than 40%, very disappointed. Whereas the companies that grew the fastest almost always had more than 40%, very disappointed."
— Rahul Vohra, Lenny's Podcast (00:55:30)

"So it's a function of both switching costs and utility of the product."
— Sean Ellis, Lenny's Podcast (00:21:51)

"I don't believe we have product market fit unless 50% of Brazilians are telling us they would be very disappointed, because Brazilians are inherently polite."
— Jag Duggal, Lenny's Podcast (00:15:22)

"We rarely scale a project, a product we've launched, until we know the Sean Ellis score and we know that it's hit a threshold that we find really compelling."
— Jag Duggal, Lenny's Podcast (00:15:22)

"I don't like NPS for this, and I have a whole blog post on this. I like Sean Ellis's question of how disappointed would you be if this product disappeared?"
— Sarah Tavel, Lenny's Podcast (00:57:39)

## Acting on the survey

"you want more people to be very disappointed without your product. The trick here is not to act too much on the feedback that the very disappointed people are giving you, because they already love your product."
— Rahul Vohra, Lenny's Podcast (00:56:03)

"just ignore the people who say they'd be somewhat disappointed. They're telling you it's a nice to have. They're as good as gone, so just ignore those guys."
— Sean Ellis, Lenny's Podcast (00:40:43)

"what is the benefit that my must-have users are focused on? And then of the users who say they'd be somewhat disappointed, so the nice-to-have users, of those users who are also focused on that benefit, what do they need in the product for it then to become a must-have for them?"
— Sean Ellis, Lenny's Podcast (00:41:54)

"people who use it this way are like 60% likely to be very disappointed without the product, but people who use it this way are 35% likely to be very disappointed, but way more people use it the 35% way."
— Sean Ellis, Lenny's Podcast (00:34:35)

"And so it's really the combination of those two things. It's set the right expectations and then speed to value."
— Sean Ellis, Lenny's Podcast (00:11:38)

"So to be a must-have, it needs to be both valuable and unique."
— Sean Ellis, Lenny's Podcast (00:44:45)

"extreme product-market fit is a state of widespread demand for a product that satisfies a critical need and crucially can be delivered repeatably and efficiently to each customer."
— Todd Jackson, Lenny's Podcast (00:17:03)

"product-market fit, it happens in the sequence of levels, it happens over multiple years."
— Todd Jackson, Lenny's Podcast (00:19:42)

"the marginal customer should be getting easier and easier and easier to get, easier to acquire them, easier to give them good service with a good product."
— Todd Jackson, Lenny's Podcast (00:18:59)

"They're basically with their products, giving away $2 for $1 and it gets them pretty far. But that's not real product-market fit."
— Todd Jackson, Lenny's Podcast (00:18:59)

"the hallmark of level two is you've got a product that a handful of people like. It's satisfying a critical need for them. Now you've got to open the demand floodgates"
— Todd Jackson, Lenny's Podcast (00:43:12)

"To get to 25 and to get beyond 25, the product has to be doing a lot of the heavy lifting for you."
— Todd Jackson, Lenny's Podcast (00:39:19)

"Let's say you've got four or five customers, but the most important feature is actually different for each one of them. That starts to look a little bit more like a consulting business than a product business."
— Todd Jackson, Lenny's Podcast (00:28:24)

"greater than 50%, probably closer to 60 or 70%, are going to get stuck at L1 or L2."
— Todd Jackson, Lenny's Podcast (01:05:52)
