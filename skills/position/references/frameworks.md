# Frameworks: position

Grouped by theme. Originators credited; all from Lenny's Podcast episodes.

## A. The Dunford method (core positioning)

1. **Definition of positioning** (April Dunford, April Dunford) — How your product is the best in the world at delivering some value that a well-defined set of customers care a lot about. Bundles alternatives, differentiation, value, target customer, category. Positioning is an input to messaging and brand, not a substitute.
2. **Obviously Awesome five-step sequence** (April Dunford, April Dunford / EOY Review) — Competitive alternatives, unique capabilities, value themes, best-fit customers, market category. Starting with category is backwards. Best for B2B tech, growth-stage startups. Steps: list alternatives; list capabilities they lack; translate to value and cluster into 2-3 themes; define accounts that care most; pick the category that makes the value obvious.
3. **Competitive alternatives / two sets of competitors** (April Dunford, April Dunford 2.0) — Ask sales what the customer does if you did not exist. Status quo (spreadsheet, manual work, intern) plus shortlist vendors plus indecision. About 40% of B2B deals are lost to no decision. Fails if you only list lookalikes.
4. **Differentiated value: capability to so-what to value** (April Dunford, April Dunford 2.0) — For each capability alternatives lack, ask so what until you reach value others cannot deliver. Cluster into 2-3 themes. Worked examples: LevelJump (built on Salesforce, to integrated data, to measuring ramp and revenue), Help Scout (high-touch human service that deepens loyalty; fits DTC and e-commerce, not call-center-cost buyers). If you have happy customers, the value already exists. Different from the internal 'why everybody loves us' list, which yields untestable opinions.
5. **Best-fit customer from value** (April Dunford) — Derive after value: which accounts care a lot about it? Do not assume up front. Camille Ricketts adds: look at who really gets it, pays more and talks about you unprompted, not who you wish they were.
6. **Actionable B2B segmentation vs personas** (April Dunford) — Personas describe a type of buyer; segments split the market. In B2B, segment by company attributes you can build lists from (team size, budget, installed tools, situation). Find what companies that love you have in common.
7. **Market category last** (April Dunford) — Category is the context that makes your value obvious to these buyers. Test candidates after value and target are settled.
8. **Niche play in an existing category, then expand** (April Dunford) — Easiest early traction (Salesforce as CRM for very small businesses). At scale (roughly 200-400M revenue) extend the category boundaries (Qualtrics, Snowflake). Endorsed by Todd Jackson and Zoelle Egner.
9. **Positioning thesis / loose fishing net** (April Dunford) — Pre-launch or a few customers: document the thesis, launch with broader language, watch who loves it and why, tighten later. The thesis is partially incorrect essentially always. Fails if you over-tighten before data.
10. **Pattern-recognition trigger to tighten** (April Dunford) — When customers who love you share a thread (same tool, same rep count) and you can predict which pitches win, narrow positioning and accelerate.
11. **Positioning workshop as team sport** (April Dunford; Claire Butler) — Marketing, product, sales, CS and CEO in one room until all agree. A week-long series with prep, a couple of days in-house. Butler: put the positioning on a big screen and have founders agree word for word; avoid two brands for one product. Hire an outsider if the exec team cannot agree, stakes are high, or for confidence.
12. **Positioning decays** (April Dunford) — Schedule check-ins; no metric will tell you it broke.

## B. Diagnostics

13. **Sales-call smell test** (April Dunford) — Listen in on first calls for: asks to repeat the pitch, mislabels ('you're just like Salesforce'), 'I can do that in a spreadsheet'. Worst case is the prospect who thinks they understand you but categorize you wrongly. No single metric flags weak positioning; it drags the whole pipeline (response, mid-funnel, churn).
14. **Ten-person test** (Arielle Jackson) — Ask about 10 employees and customers what the company does. Multiple answers means a positioning problem. Have the founder write a paragraph and iterate to one sentence.
15. **Qualified-prospect test** (April Dunford) — Judge by whether a qualified prospect gets excited, not whether a layperson understands the homepage. Applies especially to deeply technical products.
16. **Triggers for needing a narrative** (Andy Raskin; April Dunford) — Around Series B when founders cannot attend every call; new product units; pivots; a founder who pitches brilliantly while new execs tell it differently; a market that moved.
17. **Playing to win coin-flip test** (Roger Martin) — If customers could flip a coin between you and a rival, or a rival holds a lower price you cannot match, you are not winning.
18. **Zone of benefit** (Oji Udezue) — Must be roughly 2-3x better than the status quo to be noticed; a 20% gain is not.
19. **Pitch underperforming means positioning is weak** (April Dunford) — Redo positioning before polishing the pitch.

## C. Narrative and pitch

20. **Strategic narrative: old game to new game** (Andy Raskin) — Name the shift, the stakes, the object (rallying-cry question), the obstacles, and how you help. One story across marketing, sales, roadmap, fundraising, recruiting. B2B, group buyers, complex fast-changing products. Fails for consumer.
21. **The arrogant doctor** (Andy Raskin) — Anti-pattern: problem, my solution, why better than other treatments. Sets you up to brag and compare features.
22. **Name the shift concisely** (Andy Raskin) — Two or three words; accept overstatement; do not copy another deck. Examples: software to cloud, transactions to subscriptions, opinions to reality.
23. **Name the stakes** (Andy Raskin) — Show winners already in the new game; frame the future as a fork between very bad and very good. Fails if stats feel disingenuous.
24. **Reframe problems as obstacles** (Andy Raskin) — Set up new game and stakes first, then list new challenges, then show how you overcome each.
25. **Movement over category; no template** (Andy Raskin) — Category name is optional shorthand; slide count varies; these are principles. Examples: 360Learning (top-down learning to upskilling from within, no more how-are-you-different questions), Drift (world of forms to world of now), Gong (opinions to reality).
26. **Pitch setup: insight** (April Dunford) — Open with your point of view on the market, the problem inside the problem, phrased so the target nods. If they fundamentally disagree, disqualify.
27. **Reverse-engineer the insight** (April Dunford) — Start from differentiated value and ask what the buyer must know for it to matter.
28. **Positioning-to-sales-narrative and storyboard** (April Dunford) — Insight with stats, how alternatives do it differently and worse, your value. Storyboard in the workshop; marketing and sales build deck, demo or script; test on qualified prospects.
29. **Arm the champion** (April Dunford) — Prepare talking points for IT, purchasing, legal, end users and the economic buyer.
30. **Challenger insight** (Matt Dixon) — Two questions: why buy from you rather than a competitor (something only you can do), and what would have to be true for the buyer to pay a premium. Lead with the customer's hidden business problem; Dentsply won with hygienist injuries and workers' comp costs before revealing the cordless wand.
31. **Buy to avoid pain** (Jeanne DeWitt Grosser) — About 80% buy to avoid pain or reduce risk; frame around falling behind or missing targets. Fails for visionary audiences.
32. **Sell growth, not savings** (Jason Cohen) — Reframe halving AdWords cost as doubling leads; capture about 8x more revenue in his example.

## D. Copy and messaging

33. **The Bar Test** (Arielle Jackson) — Role-play two target customers at a bar; any line nobody would say aloud gets rewritten.
34. **Positioning questions to four-part statement** (Arielle Jackson) — Who is it for, their problem, how they solve it today, what you make, what a user would tell a peer; distill to: for [audience] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [benefit], unlike [old way], it works differently. Feels like mad libs without upstream work. Do it before naming, website or code.
35. **Benefit as H1** (Arielle Jackson) — The line a customer would repeat to a peer. Square Stand example: foil is the ugly old cash register, benefit is turn your iPad into a point of sale.
36. **Concentric circles of audience** (Arielle Jackson) — TAM down to a target audience you will acquire for the next 18 months, then one named model persona. Pick an audience big enough that winning most of it makes a giant business.
37. **Swappable one-liner test** (Meltem Kuran Berkowitz) — If it works on a competitor's site, rewrite it.
38. **Messaging hierarchy from survey counts and the messaging guide** (Georgiana Laudi) — About 100 survey responses; count first, second, third values; reflect their language. Deliver a 5-7 page guide: value prop, competitive advantages, value themes (emotional and functional), tied to attributes and the job to be done. One job first (a social tool lifted website conversion 89% and trial-to-paid 40%).
39. **Cocktail party test** (Rahul Vohra) — Interview hundreds, find a recurring dissatisfaction (speed), check it is unique, available and reinforcing against incumbents, then watch how people pitch you at parties.
40. **Internal jargon vs how buyers search** (Raaz Herzberg) — Check what buyers Google and use that.
41. **Combine two existing ideas** (Stewart Butterfield) — New ideas are almost impossible to plant; pair two the audience knows (Uber for Pets). Also: communicate the outcome, not the saddle.

## E. Differentiation and competitive framing

42. **Foundation Sprint differentiation two-by-two** (Jake Knapp, John Zeratsky) — Start with classic scales (fast to slow, easy to hard, free to expensive, focused to one size fits all, simple to complicated, integrated to siloed). Write custom differentiators as a new lens on the world. Score honestly, silently vote, plot competitors one at a time, keep two you can deliver and customers care about, with you alone top-right. Single-person two-by-twos for decks are worthless; honesty about where you lag is healthy.
43. **Force a choice, not a comparison** (Mike Maples Jr.) — Be the first banana among apples; spend time only on customers who value the difference.
44. **Turn the incumbent's strength into a weakness** (Mike Maples Jr.) — Do not attack; show the weakness inside the strength (Airbnb live like a local versus hotel consistency).
45. **Cut-through-the-clutter differentiator** (Ayo Omojola) — One crisp answer to why not the incumbent, repeated across products (Cash App: instant money). Fails when incumbents converge on features.
46. **Pressure = force / surface area** (Lulu Cheng Meservey) — Narrower audience, sharper message; win about 10 diehards, then expand; do not water down for people who will never like you. Underdog playbook: build your own distribution.
47. **Don't be better, be different** (Jen Abel) — Better is undefined and buyers will make you define it; once you are one of three they test you, you have mostly lost.
48. **Beachhead: big enough to matter, small enough to lead, fits crown jewels** (Geoffrey Moore) — Path to about 100M in five years, 30-50% share in two, define by geography, industry, profession, compelling use case. Positioning formula: leaders in technology who specialized and committed to this problem, respecting incumbent domain knowledge and technology competitors.
49. **Start narrow / triple-intersection niche** (Ryan Hoover; Tobi Lutke) — Narrow audience makes product, messaging and go-to-market easier; nail the intersection for a thousand true fans.
50. **Product-marketing fit before product** (Tara Seshan) — Pitch about a hundred prospects and refine the narrative before committing to product shape. Retool (Nabeel Qureshi) saw replies from CTOs after one subject-line reframe.
51. **Intercom four jobs** (Bob Moesta) — Products are hired for conflicting jobs; turn off irrelevant features per pathway and match pricing and positioning to the alternative each job replaces.
52. **Create your own wall** (Ivan Zhao) — Horizontal products need named solutions or segments to give go-to-market a clear place in minds.

## F. Category decisions

53. **Category creation readiness test** (Barbra Gago) — Budget line, customer vocabulary, competition, scope. Greenhouse abandoned its new category when buyers kept saying applicant tracking. A category is not real until multiple companies claim it; try fast, abandon fast; waves follow inflection points.
54. **Dual-track and umbrella category** (Barbra Gago) — Rate yourself in the established category while educating on the larger one (Miro visual collaboration; performance management and employee progression). Look at G2 and Software Advice groupings.
55. **Join an existing category and be much better** (Todd Jackson) — Ironclad moved from an AI legal assistant to a better CLM where buyers were already spending.
56. **Elevate a profession, not a category** (Zoelle Egner) — Build community around the job; a job is an identity.
57. **Category point of view / from-to** (Christopher Lochhead) — Frame, name and claim the problem and educate on the shift. Opposes positioning-against-competitors; see debates in SKILL.md.
58. **Incumbent framing in press** (Emilie Gerber) — Doing what a household-name incumbent does, but better, gives reporters a frame; category-creation language does not land.
59. **If you're explaining, you're losing** (Laura Modi) — Do not convince the committed opposition or point at alternatives as worse; amplify those who need you.
60. **Position on a shifted reality** (Sanchan Saxena, Meltem Kuran Berkowitz) — Airbnb pivoted to safety versus hotels in COVID; Deel positioned as global work, not remote work, to survive the trend ending.
