# Pricing frameworks (from 79 Lenny's Podcast guests)

Grouped by job. Each entry: what it is for, when it applies or fails, steps, benchmark, and who else endorses it.

## A. Choosing the pricing model

**1. Attribution x Autonomy 2x2** (Madhavan Ramanujam, episode: Madhavan Ramanujam 2.0) — Picks an AI pricing model on two axes: attribution (can you prove value on their KPIs) and autonomy (is a human in the loop). Applies to AI products. Fails if you rush to outcome-based without provable attribution.
Steps: low/low = seat or subscription; high attribution/low autonomy (Cursor-style copilot) = hybrid seats plus consumption credits; high autonomy/low attribution (autonomous infra) = usage-based; high/high (Intercom Fin) = outcome-based. High attribution plus high autonomy is where pricing power lives.
Benchmark: about 5% of companies are in true outcome-based pricing today, expected to reach 25% in three years; outcome-based AI firms capture 25-50% of value versus 10-20% typical for SaaS.

**2. Roadmap to outcome-based pricing** (Madhavan Ramanujam, Madhavan Ramanujam 2.0) — For AI products on seats or hybrid today. Steps: understand the customer's KPIs, productize features that show you move them, build value-attribution dashboards, run ongoing value audits, build agentic workflows to remove the human from the loop.

**3. Outcome pricing from value, not cost** (Eoghan McCabe, Eoghan McCabe; endorsed by Bret Taylor) — Intercom charges 99 cents per resolved ticket, derived from what customers pay per human-resolved ticket ($20-30 for SaaS, about $5 consumer), not from the AI's cost (it started around 120 cents of cost per dollar and fell). Steps: align revenue to the outcome customers value, research current spend per outcome, test price points ($10, $5, $2.50 were considered), pick the nexus between your earnings and palatability, treat cost as your problem. Bret Taylor: pre-negotiate a rate per outcome; usage and outcomes are not necessarily correlated in AI. Fails when impact cannot be attributed or the agent is not autonomous. Brian Balfour warns outcome margins get competed away unless paired with a durable moat (proprietary data, network effects).

**4. Subscription vs usage decision criteria** (Madhavan Ramanujam, Madhavan Ramanujam) — Subscription when customers want predictable bills, usage is steady, value is ongoing even if usage is episodic (LifeLock), or simplicity helps (Spotify). Usage when customers want low commit, fairness, intermittent value, or costs scale with usage. Hybrid like HubSpot is valid. Do not pick the in-vogue model; usage pricing without trackable, agreed metrics is a bad idea.

**5. Hybrid fixed plus usage quota** (Naomi Ionita) — Only about 5% of SaaS is pure usage; most use good/better/best subscriptions with quotas on a value metric because CFOs want predictability. Steps: fixed tier with a quota on the value metric, hitting the quota is the upgrade trigger or overage.

**6. Value metric first** (Patrick Campbell, Patrick Campbell; also Naomi Ionita, Melissa Tan) — The highest-leverage pricing decision. Pick a metric that scales with customer value (per user, per video, per thousand visits) so accounts upgrade or downgrade automatically. Benchmark: churn about 20-25% lower, expansion roughly doubled. Fails for physical goods. Melissa Tan: not choosing a metric early forces painful grandfathering later. Examples: Segment moved from APIs to monthly tracked users, seen as fairer.

**7. Value matrix** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Two-dimensional pricing (seats and departments) with per-user price falling as both grow, incentivizing wall-to-wall adoption for PLG expansion.

**8. Skimming, penetration, maximization** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Only three pricing strategies: skimming (launch high, Apple), penetration (thin margin volume, Amazon), maximization (between, Microsoft). Choose one and build products and business model around it. Penetration is a fallacy unless your cost structure supports it.

**9. Maker billing / free viewers** (Shishir Mehrotra, Shishir Mehrotra; Claire Butler) — Charge only creators, not editors or viewers, so there is no price friction at the share edge. Figma charges editors; viewers and commenters are free, so the product spreads to PMs for free.

**10. Create value, then split it** (Jason Cohen) — Expansion comes from first creating more value, then taking a portion through tiers, seats or usage that rise automatically, not from adding features and making customers pay.

**11. Marketplace pricing: incrementality and cost allocation** (Jason Droege; Nilan Peiris) — Droege: price off the value of incremental demand to the supplier (a restaurant tripling volume sees ~70-80% incremental gross margin), then back into take rate (Uber Eats started at 30%), delivery fee and driver pay. Peiris: allocate every bill to the transaction that caused it, add margin, raise prices on the costliest 20% and cut for everyone else while engineering costs down (people, realized risk, partner fees). Ramesh Johari: a flat percentage can trap you as relationships mature and invites disintermediation; Dan Hockenmaier: commission has no simple demand curve. Noam Lovinsky: raising prices into a liquidity problem is a local maximum.

## B. Finding the price (willingness to pay)

**12. Van Westendorp** (Rahul Vohra, Rahul Vohra; Naomi Ionita; Nick Turley; Grant Lee) — Ask four questions: so expensive you would not consider it; so cheap you doubt quality; starting to get expensive; bargain. Plot four curves. Position first, then anchor on starting-to-get-expensive if best-in-class; marketplaces and land-grabs orient to the bargain price. Superhuman asked about 100 users and landed at $30 a month, then gut-checked that 300K subscribers reaches $1B. Nick Turley's ChatGPT $20 came from a Google Form posted to Discord. Grant Lee (Gamma) paired it with conjoint and shipped one ~$20 plan.

**13. Acceptable / expensive / prohibitively expensive** (Madhavan Ramanujam; Todd Jackson) — Quick method for Monday morning. Pitch value, then ask the three prices. At scale, look for cliffs (20-30% more calling it expensive crossing 99 to 101). Todd Jackson: the expensive answer is what people actually pay, fair is deal-seeking.

**14. Relative value plus price-sensitivity questions** (Julia Schottenstein) — Customers will not say what they will pay, so anchor on a related spend (dbt versus warehouse cost; customers value it at 20-35%) and ask what is very cheap, fair, and too expensive. Have the pricing conversation before you build, not when sales cannot close; stars and usage are not a revenue proxy.

**15. Trade-off (conjoint) exercises** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Show realistic buying scenarios with varied features, plans and prices, with a none option, to reveal mental models and simulate elasticity. Heavier; best for late-stage products needing precision.

**16. Range-of-prices fake door** (Mike Maples Jr., Chegg) — Build a fake site that 404s at checkout and show a spread of prices up to well above the hypothesis (Chegg went to $75 on a $35 guess). Plot the demand curve and look for the surprise.

**17. Birkin bag exercise** (Anish Acharya) — Price is a measure of product-market fit; ask what the $1,000 or $10,000 a month version would be and what it must do. Consumer AI that must be free is outdated.

**18. Pay-what-you-want slider in beta** (Karri Saarinen) — Linear's optional per-seat slider (up to $20) tested payment and gave directional confidence; it did not give enough data to set the price.

**19. Value stories and reference frames** (Madhavan Ramanujam; Tony Fadell; Ravi Mehta; Bob Moesta) — Translate price into a small familiar unit (Superhuman: a dollar a day for four hours back a week). Nest priced at $249, five to six times a normal thermostat, because it saved $800-1,200 a year. Tinder priced Platinum against dating spend, not other subscriptions. Moesta raised a condo's price and bundled moving plus two years of storage, lifting sales over 30%. Moesta also warns that overshooting what the customer wants invites price discounts.

**20. Beautifully simple pricing test** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Ask early customers to explain your pricing back as if selling for you. If they cannot, simplify.

**21. Price is a measure of value; price paralysis** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Reluctance to raise prices is internal and emotional; a company that cannot raise price 10% has a terrible business.

## C. Packaging and tiers

**22. Leaders, fillers and killers** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Run most/least ranking. Must-have-and-pay are leaders, nice-to-have are fillers (bundle for marginal price), not-needed are killers (sell as add-ons or they spoil the bundle). Thresholds: 10-20% want it badly means add-on; more than 50% means leader.

**23. Day one vs day 100 premium features** (Naomi Ionita) — Put day-one-value features in the starter paid plan, and advanced or data-scale-dependent features in pro as an upsell (Invoice2go doubled upgrade rate while raising pro price about 30%). Avoid the laundry-list matrix.

**24. Compromise effect and decoy** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Move the middle tier to the inelastic threshold and add an expensive decoy so the middle wins (79 to 99, 149 to 199, add 299: 30%+ MRR/ARPU lift, no product change). Hold features back from the entry tier; if 60-70% pick it, you gave the farm away.

**25. Productize to segments** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Build different products and packages for different needs (Eventbrite: one ticket type, unlimited, enterprise). Positioning one product to every segment is one size fits none.

**26. Benefit-named limits and benefits-based pricing page** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Shopify names limits by benefit (inventory locations); SmugMug's rewrite from features to benefits yielded a double-digit revenue gain. Show annual plans as a monthly price.

**27. SKU audit by who buys** (Jeanne DeWitt Grosser) — Vercel found half its Enterprise SKU buyers were startups, so it split those features out for self-serve and fed the PLG funnel.

**28. Multi-product packaging** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Choose platform plus add-ons, good/better/best versions, use-case packaging, or fully modular; plan cross-sell and upsell.

**29. Free / Pro / Org tier design** (Claire Butler) — Free to pro must be natural and card-based with no humans; pro to org is organizational-scale features that sales sells (design systems). Most sales leads come from pro and free. Revisit packaging at every launch.

**30. Rebalance the whole package** (Naomi Ionita) — Impact comes from rebalancing pricing and packaging against the journey (single-player, team, wall-to-wall), not one new price or plan. Also: 20-80 axiom (Madhavan Ramanujam): 20% of what you build drives 80% of willingness to pay and is often the easiest 20%; do not give it away.

**31. Razor blade and de-featured alternatives** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Razor blade: attractive platform price, monetize consumables, because buyers scrutinize upfront cost. De-featured version: keep a cheaper stripped option rather than discounting.

**32. Open-core split** (Julia Schottenstein) — Keep the open standard open; monetize stateful interactions and cross-team collaboration. Value creation over value capture: dbt charges a small fraction of perceived value by design.

## D. Free tier, trials and conversion

**33. What to make free** (Naomi Ionita; Elena Verna; Ben Williams) — Free: the aha moment and habit path, anything driving virality or network effects, commodity features. Paid: friction for the growth model, cost-prohibitive features. Define the driver to move between plans (Snyk: governance and compliance). Question: how does free help my growth model outcomes without sacrificing monetization potential?

**34. Three pillars of free-to-paid** (Elena Verna) — In order: monetization awareness (feature walls, usage walls, trials), conversion rate optimization (pricing page, checkout, payment methods), monetization model changes (most cross-functional, last). About 75% of free users do not know what the paid plan sells.

**35. Rule of three paid triggers** (Elena Verna) — Visually consistent paid triggers, each user sees at least three (Slack message limits, SurveyMonkey gold UI).

**36. Reverse trial** (Lauryn Isford; Albert Cheng) — Premium for 7-30 days then fall back to free; do both freemium and trial. Strongest for B2B with lock-in and when brand reach matters. You only get a couple of moments to ask for a card or call before losing trust.

**37. Sample paid inside free** (Albert Cheng, Grammarly) — Intersperse a capped number of paid suggestions in the free experience; nearly doubled upgrades. Instrument paywall hits, refresh the cap. Fails where marginal cost is high.

**38. Limit files, not collaborators** (Claire Butler, Figma) — Flip the free tier so collaboration is unlimited and files are capped; delay payment until teams build advocacy. Sean Ellis: free must be good enough for word of mouth and premium distinct enough to upgrade.

**39. Guilt-driven conversion diagnosis** (Naomi Ionita) — Survey converters on why they upgraded; if guilt is a top reason, the free tier is too good and one premium tier leaves money on the table. Interview avid and new users separately and build persona plans. Also: revenue-for-growth trade (Figma) only works when you have a bridge up-market.

**40. Trial design** (Ben Williams; Merci Grace; Jason Lemkin) — Question time-boxed trials (usage-limited, or richer free plan); cohort non-converting low-engagement trial users into an enhanced free plan to test. Longer trials convert more because buying timing is the buyer's. Offer dynamic trial lengths for large or regulated customers. Lemkin: use the longest customer-centric trial; 14 days came from Salesforce wanting to close in-month; let SMBs choose monthly or annual.

**41. Checkout and pricing-page hygiene** (Hila Qu) — Benchmark checkout against top e-commerce; segment success by country and add local payment providers (India fix); do not force a quote request in a self-serve flow.

**42. Conversion benchmarks** (Yuriy Timen; Elena Verna) — Free-to-paid under 5% will not sustain a company, aim above 7%. Consumer subscriptions at $5-7 cap near $50-60 LTV versus hundreds for prosumer products. Self-serve monetization caps near $10,000 a transaction.

## E. Changing prices

**43. Raise until something moves** (Jason Cohen) — Raise substantially, watch signups; if flat, raise again before spending the margin. Price selects the market (mesa demand curve). Example: $300 a year tool raised 12x with signups unchanged. Fails if the new market needs SOC 2, integrations or services.

**44. Annual increase as forcing function** (Patrick Campbell) — If NPS is over 20, raise once a year; the exercise forces data, sales enablement and messaging alignment.

**45. Earn the increase** (Jason Lemkin) — An 8% raise should come with about 30% more value; ship that value first.

**46. Pricing committee and quarterly change** (Patrick Campbell; Naomi Ionita) — Revenue per customer as the single KPI; two founders at a tiny company, about eight people at a huge one (PM, data science, growth for PLG; sales, finance, rev ops for enterprise); one change per quarter on a recurring calendar invite (price, packaging, add-ons, discounts, localization, freemium). Naomi: treat pricing like the roadmap, revisit every 6-12 months with launches. Madhavan Ramanujam: model review yearly in AI, price points every 6-12 months, prices rise 3-5% a year. Benchmark (Naomi Ionita citing OpenView): about half of companies that changed pricing saw at least 25% ARR increase.

**47. Downturn playbook** (Madhavan Ramanujam; Sahil Mansuri; Chris Miller) — Keep a de-featured alternative instead of discounting; trade value (extra product, multi-year terms, payment terms 15 to 30 days) before cutting price; use the downturn to switch to usage pricing; try radical repricing (monthly or even daily billing) rather than shaving price. HubSpot used goodwill pricing in COVID to grow free and starter.

## F. Enterprise and sales-led pricing

**48. 75-150K initial contract and deal-size math** (Jen Abel) — Enterprises are used to a 75-150K first contract; contain scope, show years two and three. Prefer one 100K deal over ten 10K deals (discounting, procurement drag, wrong-fit customers). A 10K to 15K expansion is 50% growth; 100K to 500K is natural. Pricing an enterprise at small-business rates documents a price you cannot later step up.

**49. Co-author the price with the champion** (Jen Abel) — Hold price until post-demo, ballpark if pressed, ask how they will defend it and where they would cut, trade price for term length or a year-two step-up, never negotiate with yourself.

**50. Win-rate and sales-cycle tests** (Jen Abel; Todd Jackson) — Qualified-opportunity win rate of 25-35% is healthy; higher means underpriced. 90-day cycle supports about $100K; nine months, $250-300K. Slow cycle plus low ACV is the quadrant of death. Naomi Ionita: keep asking for more, accept losing 20-30% on price, push incrementally.

**51. Outcome-anchored enterprise pricing** (Nabeel S. Qureshi) — Price against the value of the mission outcome (a $100M plane issue), not comparable tooling; the ticket funds embedded engineers. Fails for commodity buyers comparing feature price. Jen Abel: services budget is always there.

## G. AI-specific pricing

**52. Low-price anchor trap** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — A $20 AI product to grab share trains customers to expect cheap; anchor on value and pair land with an expansion strategy. Monetize from day one; test and learn.

**53. Usage tiers for agentic AI** (Eric Simons) — Flat low pricing breaks when inference is costly; users exhausted a $9 plan in 48 hours and asked to pay more; tiers shipped in under a week.

**54. Frontier tier** (Nick Turley) — When the mid tier loses early access, create a much higher tier ($200) for expensive frontier capability; accept the awkward jump. The original subscription also served to demand-gate serious users.

**55. Bring your own agent** (Dan Shipper) — If users bring their own agent to operate your product, they pay the tokens and your margins hold. Elena Verna's contrarian opposite for hypergrowth: treat LLM cost as marketing and optimize engagement retention, not revenue.

## H. Strategy and traps

**56. Six archetype traps** (Madhavan Ramanujam) — Disruptors land but do not expand; moneymakers nickel-and-dime into a price-premium paradox; community builders train loyal customers to expect more for less. Borrow the other archetypes' strengths.

**57. Long-term greedy** (Peter Sellis) — Tomorrow's revenue depends on the ROI you deliver customers today; use it to justify trading short-term revenue.

**58. Complexity creep and simple pricing** (Eoghan McCabe; Paul Adams; Jason Fried) — Align price to value plus incremental add-ons produced too many models; simplifying cost Intercom about $50M ARR but made customers stay. Jason Fried's extreme: one flat price capped at $299 for unlimited users, no sales or tiers (gives up whale revenue).

**59. Allowable CAC from revenue** (Jonathan Becker; Sri Batchu) — Predict revenue per customer to back into max cost per click or lead (Grammarly); use contribution-margin payback (acquisition cost divided by monthly contribution margin) as the ROI metric.

**60. Ten percent revenue focus** (Ryan Hoover) — Spend about 10% of early focus on revenue; Product Hunt hit breakeven about 12 months after starting and could have years earlier. Also Tobi Lutke: add monetization levers to the room and do not pull them.
