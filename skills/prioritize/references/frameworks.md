# Prioritize: Frameworks Reference

Every distinct framework in this skill, grouped by theme. Each entry: originator (episode), what it is for, when it applies or fails, steps, benchmarks.

## A. Scoring and evidence

**1. ICE (Impact, Confidence, Ease)** (Itamar Gilad; invented by Sean Ellis, both on Lenny's Podcast) - Score ideas against the goal so selection is objective, consistent and transparent.
- Applies: flooded with ideas from founders, stakeholders, competitors and research; many people submit ideas and you must explain why one lost (Ellis).
- Fails: when people give themselves high confidence on gut feeling.
- Steps: define few clear goals; estimate impact on the North Star or team metric; estimate ease; assign confidence to both estimates; compare on one scale. Reach is already inside Impact, so RICE adds complexity (Gilad, Ellis). Detailed estimation can pay off when ideas are expensive to build (Lenny's note to Ellis).

**2. Confidence Meter** (Itamar Gilad) - Maps types of evidence to a 0-10 confidence score.
- Opinion layer (own conviction, pitch decks, thematic fit): about 0.1 of 10 at most. Peer review and back-of-envelope estimates: still guesswork. Anecdotal and market data, competitor features, surveys: medium. Build-and-test: medium to high.
- Use it to fill the Confidence term in ICE. Fails if the team ignores the ladder.

**3. Adjust impact after small tests** (Gilad) - If you show 12 customers and one likes it, lower the impact estimate, not only confidence. Lift confidence only with evidence.

**4. Match investment to confidence** (Gilad) - Cheap tests first, invest more as confidence rises; low-risk ideas (reordering settings) can ship on expert opinion. Know when to stop climbing.

**5. Let the team pick the ideas** (Gilad) - Anyone can propose, including managers and stakeholders; the team, especially the PM, runs ICE and chooses what to test first.

**6. GIST board and step backlog** (Gilad) - A per-team board with up to four key results, current ideas with ICE scores, and validation steps with owners; review at least every other week. For delivery-obsessed teams or managers who dictate the how, replace the product backlog with a backlog of validation steps (betas, previews). Builds context so the team asks fewer questions. Weak if goals and idea selection are poor.

**7. Impact in the same unit as your goal** (Matt LeMay, Impact-First Step 3) - Express each option's maximum impact in the goal's unit (e.g. users converted), roughly. If one high-effort, dependency-heavy option is the only thing that can reach the whole goal, pursue it and start the cross-team conversations early. Fails when proprietary scores lose the connection to the goal.

**8. Ignore C and E first / RICE trap** (Vijay Iyengar) - Murky confidence and effort bury high-reach, high-impact innovative bets. For those, ignore C and E for about a week with engineers and designers trying to solve them, then re-score and run RICE as usual. Aim for a mix of innovative bets, incremental bets and technical or product debt, not only the top scores.

**9. Most/least feature questions** (Madhavan Ramanujam) - Prioritize by willingness to pay. Show subsets of about 6 of 10 features; ask most important (must have, will pay) and least important (won't pay); rotate subsets and aggregate. People identify extremes well and the middle poorly. Roughly 20% of what you build drives 80% of willingness to pay. Example: Facebook's top-rated internal idea had no segment willing to pay once tested.

**10. Four-criteria opportunity scoring** (Chandra Janakiraman) - Rank 10-15 opportunity areas on expected impact, certainty of impact, clarity of levers, and uniqueness of levers (could another team build it better?). Score H/M/L as a group, debate, add up, sort, keep three.

**11. Important and addressable challenge** (Richard Rumelt) - List ambitions, list barriers for each, discard what you cannot make progress on now, pick the challenge that is both important and addressable, define action steps. If unsure it is addressable, find someone who knows.

**12. Choosing which customer job to lead with** (Gia Laudi) - Score each job on willingness to pay, urgency (painkiller not vitamin), handholding needed versus your product-led or sales-led capacity, retention or expansion potential, marketability and unfair advantage. Lead with one, defer others.

**13. Four-bucket investment: Brilliant Basics, Bread and Butter, Big Bets, Breaking Bad** (Anuj Rathi) - Tech debt and platforms; regular backlog; cross-team bigger deltas; company-redefining pivots. Ask for allocation of 100 points, write three alternatives with consequences; head of product and CEO decide as a strategy call. Big bets need a PR/FAQ and cross-team sign-up.

**14. Foundation Sprint Approach and Magic Lenses** (Jake Knapp, John Zeratsky) - List 2-4 implementation paths for the same customer and problem; plot on lenses (customer, pragmatic, growth, money, differentiation); if one wins most lenses, pick it, else pick the most important lens; name a first choice and a backup plan. About 4 hours.

**15. Opportunity Solution Tree** (Teresa Torres) - Outcome at the root, opportunity space (needs, pain points, desires), solutions, assumption tests. Keep top level to 3-7 nodes; opportunities must shrink going down. About 98% of people write opportunities as solutions; frame them specifically (what are you solving for whom). Structure the top level with an experience map of the customer's end-to-end journey.

## B. Roadmaps

**16. Now-Next-Later** (Janna Bastow) - Three buckets; granularity falls as certainty falls; date only where required. Roadmap is a prototype of strategy; value is in the roadmapping process. Fails where hard external dates (regulatory, seasonal, school year) require dated project planning: then plan earlier, add buffer, soft launch before the date.

**17. Outcome roadmaps** (Gilad) - Items framed as outcomes with a target time (by October achieve this outcome); a concrete build is dated only after a high-confidence test.

**18. Roadmap as a story with themes** (Jiaona Zhang) - Themes plus the why behind each; projects under themes, how left to the team. Update themes when you learn something major.

**19. Rolling roadmaps** - Rolling six months, 80% commitment for months 1-3 and 50% for 4-6, updated every three months (Varun Parmar). Rolling 12 months replanned every six months (Paige Costello), with explicit pivot-or-cut language. Rolling two years updated monthly with two big launches per year and nothing shipping off-roadmap (Brian Chesky; works for companies that can package big releases).

**20. JIT monthly planning** (Fiona Fung) - Monthly list of top priorities in a spreadsheet, confirmed weekly, themes refreshed every six months. For fast-changing AI teams; some projects still exceed a month.

**21. Seasons planning** (Asha Sharma) - Name the season by a secular change (prototyping, models, agents), 3-12 months; loose quarterly OKRs; squads set 4-6 week goals. Leave slack for unplanned work and for the slope of disrupting yourself.

**22. Detail inversely proportional to horizon** (Andrew Ambrosino) - Short-term work gets detail; nine-month plans stay hazy because precision is false when models shift.

**23. Work backwards from capability** (Nick Turley) - Look at what the new model can do and ask the most awesome way to productize it; replan around it. Run classic customer-driven work alongside for shared primitives and enterprise compliance. Do not apply a conventional framework to this mode.

**24. Public roadmap and secret roadmap** (Gaurav Misra; endorsed by Varun Mohan) - Public: user requests that every competitor also hears. Secret: unrequested ideas from your unique understanding that change behavior; launch without pre-announcing. Biggest wins come from the secret one. Fails if viral use cases are one-time.

**25. Struggle-based roadmap** (Bob Moesta) - Features only for 90-120 days; beyond that list struggling moments, since many solutions can fit one struggle.

**26. Roadmap drift check** (Maggie Crowley) - Lay the roadmap against strategy and count the share of resources on off-strategy items; delayed legacy work and special requests accumulate.

**27. Roadmap Week** (Emily Kramer) - Open cross-functional roadmap meetings before each quarter so marketing and others see the big trade-offs. May not scale forever.

## C. Goals and OKRs

**28. Radical Focus OKR core** (Christina Wodtke) - One objective with about three triangulating KRs (hard number, quality, money); ask how do we know; 10-minute KR brainstorm; atomic unit is the weekly question of what you are doing this week toward the outcome. Fails if used to fix a broken team: OKRs are a vitamin, not a medicine. Rollout: pilot with your best multidisciplinary team for a quarter, expand two teams at a time, then management; or pilot without the label.

**29. OKR rhythm** (Wodtke) - Monday commit, Friday celebrate; weekly status with KR confidence, last week, next week, max three P1s; review the top 2-5 initiatives per KR in about 10 minutes; peer review by about three teams within 24 hours instead of boss approval (Lenny doubts it scales to Airbnb size); grade loosely, retro deeply; set next quarter in the last week (about four days of planning total); leave future-quarter KRs blank.

**30. Six rules for goals** (Molly Graham) - At most three company goals; one goal wins in a fight; an intern can understand them; strategy should hurt; one goal one owner (not the CEO); follow-up process and learning. Goals are a communication tool. Seed stage: reset about every two months; annual goals only with an established GTM machine. Facebook ran on three goals for five years.

**31. Impact-First goals** (Matt LeMay) - Step 1: team goals one step from company goals, connected by one Y statement or one operator. Step 2: keep impact first at every step so it is not cascaded into oblivion. Step 3: estimate in goal units. Subtractive workshop: post-it of the team's top goal, pairs merge and shorten, compute what must be delivered against the target timeline. Test: can you trace lowest-level goals to the highest? Based on Wodtke's orbit image.

**32. Frontier of understanding** (Ravi Mehta) - Goal types by risk: understanding, dependency, execution, strategic. If forced into outcome goals, stage the quarter around learning. Hit-goal by know-why two-by-two: aim for hit and know why. Set strategy (destination) before goals. Fails with leaders who demand pure outcome goals.

**33. Execute and understand tracks** (Bangaly Kaba) - Each sprint run high-conviction execution plus 3-4 understand projects; ask for each theme what we know versus what we need to understand; include GTM and marketing in planning.

**34. Ambition tools** - Add a zero: ask what a 10x version would take (Brian Chesky). Impossible-by-effort goals that cannot be reached by working longer (Daniel Lereya; works after trust built). Crazy big goals (Melanie Perkins). Start backwards from potential (Tomer Cohen). Qualitative definition of crushing it, mostly green means unambitious (Jiaona Zhang). Uncomfortable but not doomed at about 70% attainment (Wodtke). Foil: Lane Shackleton says 70% is fine only for money-printing businesses.

**35. Fitness function and one list** (Ian McAllister; Nilan Peiris) - One metric to push up and to the right; or a single list of work that makes customers happy, ordered by impact on the hard problems.

**36. Goal ownership by team type** (Elena Verna; Ami Vora) - Product leadership accountable for the revenue target; PMs responsible for input KPIs (free-to-paid, package mix, PQA). In PLS, product owns pipeline from product, not revenue. PQA goal as conversion rate out of engaged ICP teams. Toddler soccer: do not give every team the same top-line goal; decouple into goals that ladder up.

**37. OKR alternatives** - Milestone goals (Keith Coleman and Jay Baxter); biannual one-pager plus financial plan plus roadmap (Geoff Charles, because planning took 33% of the time); headline and report card (Yuhki Yamashita); data-informed instead of KPI-driven (Tobi Lutke); seed-bet silo exempt from OKRs (Kayvon Beykpour); mission, pillars and annual goals (Melanie Perkins); charter, goals, roadmap (Eeke de Milliano). Use for small teams, speculative bets, or core experience teams. They fail where many functions must coordinate.

## D. Capacity, focus and cadence

**38. Allocation splits** - 70/20/10 (Ken Norton, Eeke de Milliano, Ronny Kohavi: expect 80% of big bets to fail); 70/30 with seeds (Alex Komoroske); 60-30-10 and minimize opportunity cost instead of ROI (Shreyas Doshi); 5-25-60 (Ryan Salva); 20-40% to architecture for platforms (Varun Parmar); three horizons with shifting splits over three years (Annie Pearl); 20-25% second-horizon time for growth (Elena Verna). Counter: no fixed quota (David Singleton, Katie Dill).

**39. Control the inbound** - Strategy as escalation filter (Shreyas Doshi); quarterly sales feature budget of about 10% of capacity (Jason Lemkin); target persona as filter (Annie Pearl); imperatives, a force-ranked list of about 10 cross-team items with a line drawn (Jeremy Henrickson); big rocks, 3-5 (Ebi Atawodi); orange and red priorities set by leadership (Hari Srinivasan); emoji-to-backlog pipeline with weekly grooming (Mihika Kapoor); name a real contactable person for each feature (Nan Yu).

**40. Planning cadence and cost** - Cap planning at about 10% of the execution period (Lane Shackleton, Gustav Soderstrom); cadence by stage: weekly at the earliest, quarterly after Series A, annual at scale (Gokul Rajaram); a real strategy cuts annual planning from weeks to about three days (Shreyas Doshi); signs the cadence is off, stale content means too slow (Claire Hughes Johnson); inverted W (David Singleton, Stripe); plan accuracy only where it pays (Geoff Charles); conservative plan with milestone-gated unlocks (Sahil Mansuri); do not plug the funnel to hit a number (Jonathan Lowenhar).

**41. Common currency growth model** (Dan Hockenmaier, Sri Batchu) - A spreadsheet model translates each team's impact into one currency so pods can be compared; keep one conceptual model plus team mini-models; funnel teams versus tension teams. Not a forecast.

**42. Sure wins plus crazy bets; diversified portfolio** (Crystal Widjaja; Noah Weiss; Laura Schaffer) - Layer certain wins with experimental bets; balance new versus existing, risky versus known, confident impact versus learning; commit annually to low/medium/high bets and expect lumpy results.

**43. Focus tests** - Most-meaningful-thing test (Daniel Lereya); alignment test of anyone's top three priorities (Rachel Lockett); hallway test of the team's OKRs (Yuhki Yamashita); explain-like-I'm-five intern test (Molly Graham).

**44. Time-box and appetite tools** - Explore options around the appetite, e.g. four versus eight weeks, to find the efficient frontier (Vijay Iyengar); ask what you would ship in two weeks; cycles with fixed automated timeboxes (Karri Saarinen).

**45. Other prioritization lenses** - Utility curves, S-shaped value of investment (Stewart Butterfield); 10x leverage rule (Mayur Kamat); two percenters, kill features used by 2% of users (Gibson Biddle); GEM force-rank growth, engagement, monetization (Gibson Biddle); customers first, pros second, company last (Noam Lovinsky); coherence and deletion over buildability (Dan Shipper); hundred dollar voting (Lane Shackleton); worst-case user then marginal user (Adriel Frederick); steepest gradient toward the North Star (Alex Komoroske); churn-reason roadmap sorted by ARR (Vijay Iyengar, short-lived).
