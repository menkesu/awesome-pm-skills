# Strategy frameworks: how to run each

Grouped by job. Originators are named; later guests who endorse or adapt a framework are noted. Episodes are Lenny's Podcast.

## A. What strategy is, and diagnosing the problem

**1. Kernel of good strategy** (Richard Rumelt, ep. Richard Rumelt; echoed by Will Larson and Jag Duggal) — A real strategy has exactly three parts: a diagnosis, a guiding policy, coherent actions. Missing one means it is something else.
- Steps: diagnose what is going on and what the challenge is; set a guiding policy (how we deal with it, including what we will not do); define actions that implement the policy and do not fight each other.
- Applies: any high-stakes challenge at any scale. Larson finds actions the smaller risk in engineering; the clarifying policy does more work. Duggal's version: name the specific customer, pain, solution and the advantage that enables it, then derive the minimal activities.
- Fails when: the diagnosis is a willful assertion (Larson), e.g. that every project can be done at once.

**2. Action agenda** (Rumelt) — Do not call it a strategy; call it an action agenda: state the problem, name what makes it hard, list the actions over six months to two or three years. Frame as things we will do, not goals we hope to hit. Best for teams stuck in mission, vision, values exercises (Rumelt: they are not prerequisites).

**3. The crux** (Rumelt) — Immerse in the problem, find the hardest part, find an insight that goes around it, build the agenda there. Do not choose from a list of common strategies; do not substitute 2x2s or five forces for problem solving. Diagnosis question: keep asking 'what makes it hard?' until the true obstacle surfaces.

**4. Foundry** (Rumelt) — 2-4 day offsite. Leader plus 7-8 people put 25-50 problems on a wall, cannot do them all, ask which are important and addressable, bring in people who know addressability, pick 1-2 and define coherent actions. For executive teams that know the issues but disagree on importance. Hard to scale as a consulting product.

**5. Asymmetry as the beginning of power** (Rumelt) — Ask how your company or team differs, what you know or possess that others do not; if needed, redefine your space smaller until the asymmetry is visible (small companies have power in a market or customer set, not worldwide). Caveat: inherited power can become a handicap when the world shifts.

**6. Strategy is integrated choices that compel customer action** (Roger Martin) — List what you control (R&D, advertising, hiring, pay), accept that customer action is the one thing you do not control, and make an integrated set of choices that compels it. Strategy is hard because it is integrative; the split where leaders do strategy and everyone else does execution is a harmful framing.

**7. Strategy as value determinants over the long term** (Hamilton Helmer) — Narrow strategy to the fundamental determinants of business value (NPV of expected cash flow). Tactical is short time constant, strategy is long. Everything falls into power, market size or operational excellence; classify each initiative and invest in the right one for your phase.

**8. Product strategy stack** (Ravi Mehta) — Five layers: mission, company strategy, product strategy, roadmap, product goals. Define top-down, debug bottom-up (missed goals: roadmap wrong? strategy unclear? company strategy not understood?). Use to explain why Tinder and Hinge diverge on overlapping features, and to decide where to copy competitors. Add wireframes to the doc (litmus test: which 4-5 items are on the nav bar).

**9. Product strategy vs business strategy** (Vikrama Dhiman) — Someone else picks the mountain; product strategy is how you climb it: segments, needs, the right product, order of work.

**10. Three-component product strategy** (Chandra Janakiraman) — Sits between mission and plan, forces choice of scarce resources. Exactly: a handful of strategic pillars, explicit non-focus areas, the why behind both. Pick three pillars, not five. Strategy has no intrinsic value; it accrues value through execution (Oculus and Portal had near-identical pillars and opposite outcomes). Strategy as resonance: find the frequency between product and market.

**11. Connect the dots / three parts of strategy** (Jackie Bavaro) — Vision, strategic framework (market, definition of success, pillars), roadmap worked backwards. Start from the target, list ~20 things the team could do, match them to the target and fill missing links (why monetize current users, not new ones). Roadmap is a feasibility check, not a commitment. Listen for confusion to find missing assumptions. New PMs: wait about six months, then draft in half a day. Repeated disagreements signal a strategy gap.

**12. Strategy coherence test and symptoms of no strategy** (Melissa Perri) — Ask each team what it does and why; stories should match and connect to customer and business value. No-strategy symptoms: 80-hour weeks with flat metrics, executives calling product a black box, teams spread thin. If you think there is none, interrogate leaders; differing answers show misalignment. Will Larson: there is always a strategy; writing it down lets you debug it.

## B. Choices, positioning and how to win

**13. Strategy choice cascade, Playing to Win** (Roger Martin, with A.G. Lafley; applied by Annie Pearl, Drew Houston, Jag Duggal) — Five linked questions: winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, capabilities, management systems.
- Steps: tentative aspiration tied to a customer lack; where to play; how to win; capabilities; systems; toggle until they reinforce each other.
- Applies: product, business unit or company. Pearl uses it to name markets, segments and personas, which forces saying where you will not play. Houston used it to be selective and lead in every market he stays in.
- Fails when: the aspiration is justified by market size, or capabilities are chosen before where to play (Martin rejects the resource-based view because it cannot say which resources to build).

**14. Where-to-play checklist** (Martin) — Which customers or parts of customers do we not yet serve; finished product or component; which channel; which value-chain stage (Four Seasons exited ownership to be excellent at management).

**15. Two ways to win and the coin-flip test** (Martin) — Be differentiated or low cost, otherwise stronger rivals push you around. If customers would flip a coin between you and a rival, find where you are clearly better or cheaper. Low cost needs dominant scale; niche cost leadership almost never happens. Differentiation is increasingly scale-sensitive (branding and R&D). Ayo Omojola's version: different, better, in a way that matters to the buyer, in a domain that matters, with working economics.

**16. Can't or won't, fault lines, too nuanced to copy** (Martin) — A how-to-win is defensible when rivals cannot build it or will not (it would hurt them, like Estee Lauder and Olay). The more interlocking your capabilities and systems, the more likely rivals decide life is too short (Westlaw headnotes). Your how-to-win is your moat; systems build and maintain it. Best of all: convince rivals to pick different wheres. Durable advantage is multifaceted; fleeting advantage is one thing only.

**17. Founding hypothesis sentence** (Jake Knapp and John Zeratsky, Foundation Sprint) — If we solve [problem] for [customer] with [approach], they will choose it over [competitors] because of [differentiator 1] and [differentiator 2]; add a backup approach. Phase one asks embarrassingly simple questions (customer, problem, competition and workarounds, advantages) with note-and-vote and a decider. Define competition broadly, including workarounds. List your unique insight, motivation and capabilities; startups cannot win on scale or distribution.

**18. Four choices** (Seth Godin) — Choose your customers (smallest viable audience: language, money, problem, temperament), competition, source of validation and distribution; decide who you are not for. Sean Ellis: prefer a passionate niche to a lukewarm broad base; align roadmap, onboarding, messaging and acquisition to the must-have segment.

**19. Hero customer** (Ayo Omojola) — Name one hero customer and resolve every trade-off in that customer's favor (Cash App picked consumers over merchants). Expect internal friction; firewall the team.

**20. Differentiation vs table stakes** (Paul Adams; Dmitry Zlokazov; Josh Miller) — Classify roadmap items as differentiation or table stakes; Intercom swung from 70/30 either way to about 50/50. Match competitors on fundamentals and remove known bad UX, then add delighters and integration synergies. Beautiful design cannot rescue failing the must-win attribute in a category. Choose a category where your strengths are the winning attributes. Fix the fire first when table-stakes gaps bleed customers (Vijay Iyengar).

**21. Four Ps** (Todd Jackson) — Persona, problem, promise, product must line up. When stuck, decide which to change: Lattice kept persona, Vanta changed all four, Plaid kept product. Pivot 200%, not 10%; sell from mockups before building when the product is simple enough.

**22. Category design and the Better trap** (Christopher Lochhead; sympathetic: Jag Duggal; counterweight: April Dunford, Todd Jackson) — Do not launch a known solution to a known problem and call it better. Solve a new problem or reframe, name and claim an existing one; compete against the status quo, not brands. Data point: in tech categories one company captures about two-thirds (76%) of value created. Caveats: category creators are often overtaken by well-funded fast followers (Dunford); incremental improvement still matters in mature products; Todd Jackson prefers entering categories where customers already spend. Mike Maples Jr. agrees that better is an extension of the present: win with a radically different future an incumbent cannot follow.

**23. Bowling pin and fish-to-pond** (April Dunford; Geoffrey Moore) — Win a segment the leader underserves, use it as a beachhead to knock over adjacent segments. Ecosystems form around market leaders, so be the big fish in a small pond first.

**24. Demand-side competitive set and choose what to suck at** (Bob Moesta) — Define competitors by the job: Snickers competes with protein drinks and coffee. Decide what to be bad at; your trade-offs must map to the customer's.

**25. DHM: delight, hard to copy, margin-enhancing** (Gibson Biddle) — Strategy is a set of hypotheses about delighting customers (aim near 10x) in ways that are hard to copy and improve margin; test one theory at a time, expect about six in ten to fail. Easy-to-copy delighters (a happy family photo) are copied within a week. Reed Hastings had him kill advertising because Netflix must be best at personalization.

**26. Core product value phrase** (Peter Sellis) — One phrase for why users return daily (Snap: fastest way to share a moment with people you care about); break each word into a metric and tie it to retention; filter the roadmap and onboard hires with it.

## C. Moats and competitive power

**27. Power = benefit + barrier, and the to-be-or-not-to-be test** (Hamilton Helmer, ep. Hamilton Helmer) — A source of power needs a benefit (lower cost or higher price than rivals) and a barrier that stops mimicry. If both exist, estimate value from durable margins; if not, it is not power. Moat is the barrier half only. Castle or shack: list what the company spends most time on and discard what can be mimicked.

**28. Seven Powers and power progression** (Helmer) — Counter-positioning, scale economies, switching costs, network economies, process power, branding, cornered resource (empirical, not theoretical; Martin doubts the list is complete). Availability differs by phase: branding and process power are off the table at the start; resource only in rights-based businesses; startups nearly always start with counter-positioning (PMF is mostly substitution), then scale, switching costs or network economies depending on relative scale. Power always tilts probabilities; start thinking about it pre-PMF. Iconic businesses have second acts (AWS) that restart power building.

**29. Counter-positioning** (Helmer; Martin's fault lines) — Without it an incumbent with the capabilities can extend its product and match you. Find a position the incumbent cannot take without hurting its existing business.

**30. Materiality test** (Helmer) — Flywheels often exist but rarely matter. Network effects are not network economies unless the value benefit supports a price or cost delta that materially changes margins. Data scale economies are rare because rivals are within shouting distance of your scale. Scale economies check: large fixed cost spread over materially more units (Netflix content is about 50% of cost). Uber lesson: define the unit where scale applies (a city). Process power must be material and opaque and not sold as best practice by consultants; operational excellence is essential to get there but is not usually power.

**31. Origination, takeoff, stability** (Helmer) — Origination: find the technology shift behind PMF. Takeoff: capture offshoots of the wave and new segments. Stability: a market-share fight that requires defending your source of power. A PM should know the business's power and surface what they see in the weeds.

**32. AI moat tests** (Anish Acharya; Scott Wu; Peter Deng; Bret Taylor; Mike Krieger; Grant Lee; Varun Mohan; Shaun Clowes; Evan Spiegel) — Moats are discovered, not designed; classic moats still apply and none depended on how hard software is to build. Stickiness: accumulated customer knowledge plus multiplayer use. Data flywheel plus workflow plus craft (Deng). Technical orchestration moats are comically narrow while the product moat is large (Taylor). Defensible for 1-3 years via domain knowledge, a differentiated buyer-persona go-to-market, new form factors and urgency (Krieger). Durable wrapper: a problem you will care about for 5-10 years, end-to-end workflow, many models per step (Lee). Build models only where usage data is unique (Mohan). Lock-in is configured business rules (Clowes). Software is not a moat; ecosystems, platforms and hardware are (Spiegel; Matt Mullenweg's 60,000 plugins). Counterpoints: Marc Andreessen, argue both sides before declaring; Michael Truell, entrenchment can be leapfrogged, so the moat is being best.

**33. Layer-economics tests for model providers** (Benedict Evans) — Utility-margin test: apps above the model do not pay a share of revenue to the commodity supplier. Windows vs AWS leverage test: if thousands of apps are the UX and models are interchangeable, expect cloud-like dynamics. Ask whether labs have pricing power at steady state. If the product is a commodity, distribution, defaults and brand decide.

**34. Competitor intelligence methods** (Patrick Campbell; Varun Parmar; Annie Pearl; Chandra Janakiraman; Elena Verna) — Lightweight program: send white-labeled surveys to rivals' customers, quarterly calls with their friendly customers, honest comparison pages if you are a challenger, keep intel away from product teams. Score competitor strength on three factors: good-enough solution, distribution outreach, pricing and packaging. War gaming: assign each group one competitor per quarter, SWOT and trajectory, presentation day. Competitive stack chart from releases. Teardowns as ideation input only, never the destination. Houston: bundlers act like a boa constrictor, so launch dates mislead and your numbers lag; anticipate the business-model attack, not just the product.

## D. Focus, portfolio and expansion

**35. Right to win / permission to play** (Jeetu Patel; Geoff Charles; Manik Gupta; Shaun Clowes) — Would it be logical to customers that you built this? Do you have a route to mass-scale distribution? Which components do you already own (Ramp reused money movement, risk, accounting integrations)? Company-product fit and sponsorship precede PMF. Zombie products with no right to win should be called dead around six months.

**36. All-in-one top-three test, dimensional complexity, three orders of cost** (Dharmesh Shah) — If you are top three in a category inside a suite you over-invested. Product two adds a dimension to every decision, chart and campaign. Price features at build cost, maintenance, and the complexity added to every future decision. Add a knob, remove a knob. Reverse gravity: markets pull software upmarket; staying SMB means few competitors but you must fight the pull.

**37. Strategic inflection: go all in** (Drew Houston; Eoghan McCabe; Vijay Iyengar; Jag Duggal) — Signals: competitors bundle, your good numbers lag, sales struggle in once-easy accounts. Pretend to be consultants to your own company; pick the one market where you can lead; cut loved products; fix the P&L first if you went into a land grab. Iyengar: after about 40% revenue churn in the core, say a hard no to adjacent categories; invest profits, not people. Mid-market dispersion avoids customer hostage-taking (Chris Miller).

**38. Portfolio frameworks** (Varun Parmar; Megan Cook; Fareed Mosavat; Kayvon Beykpour; Chris Hutchins) — Three horizons 70/20/10. Stage-gated bets: Wonder (1 person), Explore (~3), Make (~12), Impact, Scale, with a check to kill at each gate. Four types of product work: feature, growth, PMF expansion, scaling. Core plus speculative bets; sacred cows are a free roadmap. Slugging average over batting average: pick bets that would make today's business 10% of the company.

**39. Business as a math equation** (Tom Conrad) — Model how investment turns into returns over a time horizon, stress-test the big-swing assumptions, revisit when cost estimates balloon (Quibi from about 2B to 6-10B). Jiaona Zhang: Airbnb Plus ignored unit economics and operations strength.

**40. Positional vs tactical game; micro, macro, meta** (Tobi Lütke; Drew Houston) — Be good enough at tactics not to die, but value comes from position: do customers want more or less from you, are you optimized out or asked to absorb other spend. Houston: play micro (product), macro (business model, competition) and meta (cycles vs permanent shifts) at once.

## E. Vision, narrative and deployment

**41. Vision vs strategy and four elements of a good vision** (Adam Mosseri; Ebi Atawodi; Melissa Perri) — Vision is the target state; strategy is an opinionated path a reasonable person could disagree with. A good vision is lofty, realistic, free of today's tech limits and grounded in a potent user problem; concrete enough to picture, different from today, explicit about what you will not do. A tagline is not a vision.

**42. Vision artifacts** (Ebi Atawodi; Nancy Duarte; Cameron Adams) — Empathize, create, evangelize (patience: Vision 2026 took about 18 months to reach planning). Once upon a time story. Future headline. App store screenshot exercise (four blank panels). Micro-visions for every PM. Match artifact to horizon: tweet (6 months), headline or mock (1 year), macro vision (4-5 years, evergreen). Journey keyframes on the wall (Airbnb picked six to make awesome).

**43. Backcasting, ladders and peaks** (Mike Maples Jr.; Melanie Perkins; Tomer Cohen; Alex Komoroske; Christopher Lochhead) — Imagine a radically different future and work back; Column B planning; wild success vs terrible failure at 10 years; ladder to the moon with small rungs; mountain peak and base camp with a blurry middle; low-resolution North Star at 3-5 years that experts call plausible but transformative. Lead with beliefs, then prove with evidence (Cohen).

**44. Short-horizon vision for AI** (Jenny Wen; Alexander Embiricos; Tom Verrilli; Dianne Penn) — 3-6 month prototype vision; fuzzy aim 1+ years out, concrete next weeks, skip detailed 6-12 month plans; play the accordion between zooming out and shipping the smallest proof; strongly held theme, weakly held prototype.

**45. Strategy deployment ladder, missing middle, CEO two-pager** (Melissa Perri) — Executives write strategic intents; directors write problem-oriented product initiatives; teams write solutions; two pages each, linked in a wiki. The missing middle: teams know their work, executives know the goals, nothing connects them. If no strategy exists, the CEO writes a two-pager: history, threats, what to care about and not, intents for 2-3 years.

**46. Strategy documents** (Maggie Crowley; Ebi Atawodi; Ravi Mehta; Varun Parmar; Anuj Rathi) — Landscape-first doc walking from mission to team priority with a summary on top. Insights, strategy, big rocks. Top 10 things you should know: a living quarterly problem list per PM. Annual strategy white paper linked to OKRs. Strategy on a page, three alternatives so executives can align on one.

**47. Making strategy stick** (Molly Graham; Ami Vora; Nickey Skarstad; Andy Raskin; Tomer Cohen; Lane Shackleton) — Strategy should hurt. It must change what is built, funded or staffed. Build with the team but have one decider. Use the narrative as the bar for feature requests from sales. Principles have teeth. Keep strategy discussions separate from OKR setting. Leadership survey: forecast revenue mix in three years; if answers diverge, debate the ramifications.
